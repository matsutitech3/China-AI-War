\chapter{How Much Compute Does a Nation's Science Need?}
\chaptermark{Sizing Sovereign AI4S}
\label{ch:sizing}

Every argument in this book has so far been about who supplies capability and at what price. This chapter asks the reciprocal question, which almost no government has answered in public: \emph{how much does a country actually need?} It matters here for three reasons. It converts the sovereignty argument from a posture into a number, and numbers can be checked. It exposes---quantitatively---the gap between what nations are announcing and what their own research populations would consume, which turns out to be an order of magnitude or more. And it supplies the demand side of the institutional proposal in Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium}: one cannot design a federation to pool capacity without a shared, comparable estimate of the capacity required. The material here follows a measurement-anchored sizing framework developed at RIKEN R-CCS and its three national instantiations~\cite{ai4s-framework}; the epistemic discipline is the framework's own, and it matches this book's: every input is typed by what would make it trustworthy.

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Why this chapter is in the main text.} A sizing framework detailed enough to be checked can read as a second book inserted into the first, and the temptation is to relegate it to an appendix. The temptation is resisted, and the reason is Chapter~\ref{ch:prize}: if the largest value in AI is scientific, then the question ``how much compute does a nation's science need, and what does it cost to rent instead of own?'' is not a technical annexe to the convergence thesis---it is the thesis, evaluated on the workload that matters most. What the appendix instinct is right about is the connective tissue, and this chapter supplies it: every section below closes by naming the chapter it feeds.
\end{quote}

\section{Three demand regimes, and why a share in one is not evidence about another}
\label{sec:regimes}

One labelling discipline governs every demand share in this chapter and in Chapters~\ref{ch:prize} and~\ref{ch:consortium}, and it has to be stated before any percentage is printed, because the same physical capability---serving LLM tokens---carries entirely different weight in each of three regimes.

\emph{Regime~G, general and consumer AI}, reaching users through phones and applications, is essentially all LLM token generation. Agents in it call other services, but those services are public and shared---web search for deep research, a maps endpoint, a code sandbox---not privately provisioned high-end compute, and they are not what the buyer is paying to install.

\emph{Regime~E, enterprise streamlining}---accounting, personnel, approvals, document flow---is likewise essentially all token generation. The data is private and the integration work is substantial, but the provisioned capability is the same serving capability as Regime~G.

The United States and China contest G and E head to head, and every price table in Chapter~\ref{ch:model} is a statement about those two regimes.

\emph{Regime~S, AI for science and industry}, is the subject of this chapter, and it is not built the same way. It is dominated by \emph{non-LLM} workloads---simulation, training of models that are not language models, data processing at instrument rate, and integration with physical apparatus---with a token layer for agent orchestration that is a minority of the requirement and, on the scenario of Section~\ref{sec:serving-scenario}, shrinking.

Nothing in this book's numbers is contradictory once this is labelled, and a good deal of the public argument about AI infrastructure is incoherent because it is not. ``Language models are one seventh of the workload'' is a Regime~S statement and is evidence about nothing in G or E. ``Token serving is the entire business'' is a G/E statement and is evidence about nothing in S. A firm sizing a Regime~G fleet and a ministry sizing a Regime~S fleet are buying different machines for different reasons, and a demand share quoted without its regime is a number without a referent. \textbf{Every share in this chapter is a share of Regime~S demand unless it says otherwise}, and where a figure crosses regimes the crossing is named.

\section{The measurement gap}
\label{sec:measgap}

National AI-for-Science infrastructure has, until recently, been sized without measurement. Procurement scales were set by budget envelopes, vendor availability, flagship-application extrapolation, or elicitation workshops---none of which measures the per-researcher demand of the workload class the infrastructure is being built for, namely LLM agents orchestrating simulation, data analysis, and autonomous experimentation~\cite{ai4s-framework}. Meanwhile the deployment wave is global and simultaneous: the US Genesis Mission systems, EuroHPC's AI factories, the UK's Isambard-AI, Japan's Rikyu and FugakuNEXT line, and Singapore's national AI programs are all sizing sovereign AI4S capacity now, mostly without a common yardstick.

Three developments make a portable framework possible. The demand side acquired a measured anchor, and its provenance has to be printed in full, because the same raw record supports several different numbers and only one of them is the framework's driver. The record is a 56-day operational campaign of early-access agentic work on a GB200-class system (Rikyu EA1): 101 vetted users, 53,173 jobs, 340,397 GPU-hours, saturating an $\sim$800-GPU partition within five weeks. The adopted driver is
\[
w_{\mathrm{heavy}} \;=\; 443 \ \text{delivered BGPU-hours per \emph{active heavy-user day}} \quad [\mathrm{V}],
\]
measured over the seven saturation days on a \emph{delivered} basis; the same seven days on an \emph{allocation} basis give 480, and both supersede the constructive prior of 216 that the framework carried before the probe ran~\cite{ai4s-framework}. The denominator is the count of active heavy-user days \emph{inside the saturation window}. It is not the 56-day campaign across all 101 users: that naive average, $340{,}397/(56\times101) = 60.2$ GPU-hours per user-day, is a different estimand---mean intensity across an unsaturated ramp, including users who never became heavy and days on which nobody was active---and it is not the measured quantity. The two differ by a factor of about seven, which propagates directly into every national requirement, so the framework states the denominator with the number every time and so does this chapter. What the 443 figure is: a revealed-demand \emph{floor} on a saturated partition, which any country can adopt as a starting scenario and test against its own probe. What it is not: an estimate of unrationed demand, which the saturation guarantees is higher by an unmeasured margin. The supply side converged onto public, reproducible serving benchmarks. And the policy context became genuinely multi-national, so that comparability now has customers~\cite{ai4s-framework}. What the OECD's Frascati Manual did for R\&D statistics---not identical answers across countries, but commensurable ones---is what a shared, openly gated estimation framework could do for research-compute demand.

\section{A vendor-neutral unit, and a conversion rule}

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Definition (Baseline GPU).} One BGPU is the AI-serving throughput of a reference accelerator possessing an 8\,TB/s HBM memory system---concretely a B200/B300-class device. It is a \emph{planning unit}, not a product: any accelerator converts into it by the bandwidth-first, benchmark-adjusted rule below, with a stated adjustment band. A separate and never-summed unit, BGPU$_{\mathrm{BW}}$, denotes 8\,TB/s of delivered bandwidth for classical modelling and simulation. Conversion uncertainty is carried explicitly as the $\delta$ band of Table~\ref{tab:bgpu} and propagates into every capacity figure in this chapter; a $\pm$10\% band on $\delta$ moves national requirements by the same proportion, which is smaller than the population-definition sensitivity discussed below and much smaller than the behavioural-transfer uncertainty.
\end{quote}

All capacities and demands are stated in \textbf{Baseline GPUs} (BGPU): the AI-serving throughput of one NVIDIA B200/B300-class accelerator---concretely, a device with an 8\,TB/s HBM memory system, the resource that governs long-context decode. The unit is deliberately not a product. Any accelerator $g$ converts by a bandwidth-first, benchmark-adjusted rule
\[
\gamma_g \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{BW}_g}{8\ \mathrm{TB/s}} \times \delta_g ,
\]
where the bandwidth term is physics and $\delta_g$ absorbs compute-side and software-stack differences, estimated from published matched-condition benchmark results and carrying a stated band~\cite{ai4s-framework}. The rule's worked example matters politically as well as technically: AMD's MI355X carries 288\,GB of HBM3E at 8.0\,TB/s---a bandwidth ratio of exactly 1.00---and its published MLPerf record supports $\delta\approx1$, so it converts at 1.0 BGPU, largely equivalent to Blackwell. \emph{A requirement denominated in BGPU is procurable from any vendor whose devices convert at a defensible factor}, which is precisely what a public planning framework must guarantee and what a vendor-named unit cannot. Table~\ref{tab:bgpu} gives the adopted factors.

The 2030 flagship-generation factor of 6 is derived rather than taken from a vendor roadmap: dividing a published program disclosure of $\geq$800\,PB/s aggregate accelerator memory bandwidth over a disclosed fleet of $\sim$15,000--20,000 devices gives 40--53\,TB/s per device, i.e.\ 5.0--6.7$\times$ the 8\,TB/s baseline; the planning value is rounded to 6, and because the bandwidth target is a floor, these are lower bounds~\cite{ai4s-framework}. Installed fleets deliver $H_{\mathrm{yr}} = \alpha\times 8{,}760 = 7{,}884$ effective GPU-hours per GPU-year at fleet availability $\alpha=0.90$. Classical modeling-and-simulation demand, where a country carries it, is expressed in a separate bandwidth currency (BGPU$_{\mathrm{BW}}$, one unit $=$ 8\,TB/s of delivered bandwidth) and is \emph{never} summed with serving-BGPU---a discipline that prevents the most common category error in national compute accounting.

\subsection{From scalar to workload-weighted vector, and how capacities actually aggregate}
\label{sec:bgpu-vector}

The framework owes one generalization an explicit statement, and this subsection supplies it---together with a correction to the way the components are combined, because the obvious combination rule is wrong in a direction that flatters heterogeneous fleets.

A bandwidth-first scalar is defensible because long-context, memory-bound decode dominates the \emph{token-service layer}, which Section~\ref{sec:twolayer} prices at 14.3--14.6\% of Regime~S national demand. It does not dominate the other 85\%. And even within the token layer the work is a portfolio: a device strong on decode can be weak on training software, network injection, or reliability. The disciplined form therefore defines, for each device $g$, a vector of \emph{per-class throughput ratios}
\[
\mathbf{b}_g \;=\; \left( b_{\mathrm{decode}},\; b_{\mathrm{prefill}},\; b_{\mathrm{training}},\; b_{\mathrm{simulation}},\; b_{\mathrm{data}} \right)_g ,
\]
where each component is the device's sustained throughput on that workload class \emph{divided by} the reference device's sustained throughput on the same class under the matched-condition protocol of Section~\ref{sec:archtheory}---dimensionless, and equal to 1.0 for the reference device---together with three quantities that are deliberately \emph{not} members of that vector: fleet availability $\alpha_g$, injection bandwidth $\beta^{\mathrm{net}}_g$, and storage bandwidth $\beta^{\mathrm{sto}}_g$.

\textbf{The aggregation rule.} Given a fixed workload portfolio, the aggregate is not an arithmetic mean of speed ratios. Let $w_k$ be class $k$'s share of the portfolio's \emph{reference} execution time, $\sum_k w_k = 1$. The portfolio that takes one unit of time on the reference device takes $\sum_k w_k / b_{g,k}$ units on device $g$, so
\begin{equation}
G(g) \;=\; \frac{\alpha_g}{\sum_k w_k / b_{g,k}} ,
\label{eq:bgpu-agg}
\end{equation}
a reference-time-weighted \emph{harmonic} mean, with availability entering as a \emph{multiplier} outside the sum.

The counterexample is worth printing because the error it corrects is not a rounding error but a sign error. Take two classes with equal reference-time shares, one running at $b = 2$ and the other at $b = 0.5$. The portfolio takes $0.5/2 + 0.5/0.5 = 1.25$ reference-time units to finish, so the aggregate is $1/1.25 = \mathbf{0.80\times}$: on this portfolio the device is \emph{slower} than the reference and a fleet built from it needs \emph{more} devices, not fewer. The arithmetic form $\sum_k \omega_k b_{g,k}$ reports $0.5\times2 + 0.5\times0.5 = \mathbf{1.25\times}$---a 56\% overstatement that reverses the procurement conclusion. Averaging rates instead of times is the oldest mistake in performance analysis, and a national planning unit is exactly the place it must not survive.

\textbf{Why availability is a multiplier and not a row.} A fleet usable 80\% of the time delivers 0.8 of the portfolio throughput whatever the mix; averaging $0.8$ into a basket alongside a decode ratio of $1.2$ to produce a comfortable $1.0$ hides an outage behind a benchmark. The framework's $H_{\mathrm{yr}} = \alpha\times8{,}760$ already treats availability multiplicatively when converting hours to machines, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:bgpu-agg} makes the treatment consistent at the device level.

\textbf{Why network and storage are constraints, not categories.} A training job is not sometimes a network job and sometimes a compute job; it is one job whose completion time is set jointly by arithmetic throughput, collective-injection bandwidth, and checkpoint bandwidth, and the binding one changes with the parallelism plan. They therefore enter as joint derates on the classes that need them,
\[
b_{g,k} \;\longleftarrow\; b_{g,k}\,\min\!\left(1, \tfrac{\beta^{\mathrm{net}}_g}{\beta^{\mathrm{net}}_{\mathrm{req},k}}\right)\min\!\left(1, \tfrac{\beta^{\mathrm{sto}}_g}{\beta^{\mathrm{sto}}_{\mathrm{req},k}}\right),
\]
never as independent rows to be averaged against decode. Listing them as task categories lets a fabric deficiency be offset by a memory surplus, which is not something a job can do.

\textbf{What the corrected form still cannot do.} Eq.~\eqref{eq:bgpu-agg} assumes a fixed portfolio in which each class runs where it is placed. A heterogeneous fleet with a scheduler that routes work to whichever substrate suits it is not described by any single aggregate number, and pretending otherwise is the next version of the same error: the right object is a \emph{resource-allocation model}---classes, devices, a capability matrix, capacity and eligibility constraints, an objective---whose solution is an assignment and a makespan rather than a mean. Countries operating one homogeneous fleet may use the scalar; countries operating a classical fleet beside an accelerator fleet and routing between them must solve the allocation, and the framework says so rather than offering them an average.

The vector form buys what matters: a device with spectacular memory bandwidth but immature training software, poor injection bandwidth, or weak fleet reliability can no longer receive a flattering conversion factor, because $b_{\mathrm{training}}$, $\beta^{\mathrm{net}}$ and $\alpha$ are carried explicitly and the declared mix prices them. The two-layer demand model supplies the mix: the token-service layer weights decode and prefill, the scientific-execution layer weights training, simulation and data, and the published national instantiations state their $w_k$ implicitly through the layer split. The scalar survives for politics---ministries budget in one number---but it is now a declared projection of a vector under a declared mix, a declared placement and a declared availability, with all three printed beside every national requirement. (The LX2-class integrated processor is the instructive case: strong $b_{\mathrm{decode}}$ and $b_{\mathrm{simulation}}$, unproven $b_{\mathrm{training}}$, unmeasured $\beta^{\mathrm{net}}$ at fleet scale, unknown $\alpha$---Eq.~\eqref{eq:bgpu-agg} says precisely what the proof suite of Section~\ref{sec:proofsuite} must fill in before a conversion factor is defensible, and the harmonic form guarantees that a weak $b_{\mathrm{training}}$ cannot be averaged away by a strong $b_{\mathrm{decode}}$.)

\section{The two-layer demand model}
\label{sec:twolayer}

A nation's requirement for target year $Y$ is the sum of two concurrently provisioned layers, $G_{\mathrm{nat}}(Y) = G_{\mathrm{tok}}(Y) + G_{\mathrm{sci}}(Y)$: a token-service layer and a scientific-execution layer (Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-model}). Both must be provisioned simultaneously, because the agentic loop does not close otherwise---the orchestrating model is idle without the batch jobs it launches, and the batch jobs are unlaunched without the orchestrator.

\input{figs/fig-ai4s-model}

\textbf{The token layer.} The open-tier population $N$ (the number of researchers entitled to draw on the national service) is partitioned into service tiers indexed by $i$, with population shares $\varphi_i$ (summing to one), duty factors $u_i$ (the fraction of the year a researcher in tier $i$ is actively drawing tokens rather than idle), and sustained output-token rates $r_i$ while active; the portable default is a three-tier profile of 10\%/20\%/70\% of researchers at 2,000/400/150 Mtok per user-month. The national output stream is $T_{\mathrm{nat}} = N\sum_i \varphi_i\, u_i\, r_i$ tokens per second, and its serving cost is $G_{\mathrm{tok}} = T_{\mathrm{nat}}\cdot k$, where $k$ is the BGPU cost per token per second of a canonical, versioned agentic serving configuration: a \emph{master} model---a trillion-parameter MLA mixture-of-experts holding a $2^{20}$-token campaign context---delegating to cheap, high-throughput 120B-class sliding-window \emph{sub-agents} at 128K context, with 10\% of national tokens going to the master~\cite{ai4s-framework}.

That configuration produces the most consequential structural fact \emph{inside the token layer}, and Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-plots} (top) states it: at $k = 9.79\times10^{-4}$ BGPU per token per second, \textbf{88.7\% of the token-service capacity is the master term}. Ten percent of the tokens, generated at million-token context, cost nine-tenths of the token-service GPUs.

The denominator matters as much as the number, and the framework's Japanese instantiation prints all three together, so this chapter does too. Japan's token-service layer is $G_{\mathrm{tok}} = 72{,}731$ BGPU, of which the master term is 64,540 and the sub-agent term 8,191; the national requirement for the same target year is $G_{\mathrm{nat}}(2030) = 496{,}709$--$509{,}837$ BGPU~\cite{ai4s-framework}. Three shares follow, and no one of them may be quoted as another:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item the token-service layer is \textbf{14.3--14.6\% of Regime~S national demand};
  \item the master term is \textbf{88.7\% of the token-service layer};
  \item the master term is \textbf{$\approx$13.0\% of Regime~S national demand} (12.7--13.0\% across the requirement band).
\end{itemize}

The house rule from here on is that every percentage in this chapter is printed with the quantity it is a share of. The reason is mechanical. Decode throughput on one accelerator is delivered memory bandwidth divided by the bytes that must be read to emit one token, and at long context that traffic is dominated by the key--value cache. Writing $\mathrm{BW}$ for delivered bandwidth (8\,TB/s at the BGPU reference), $L_m$ for the master's resident context length in tokens, and $b_{\mathrm{KV}}$ for the KV-cache bytes per token of context, master throughput is
\[
\Theta_m \;=\; \frac{\mathrm{BW}}{L_m\, b_{\mathrm{KV}}} \;=\; 115 \ \text{tokens per second},
\]
against $\Theta_s = 8{,}161$ for the 128K-context sub-agents---a 71-fold ratio driven entirely by context length against KV traffic. That ratio has an architectural consequence for this book, and it has to be stated at the right scope. \emph{The token-service layer---one seventh of Regime~S national demand---is a long-context memory-capacity and memory-bandwidth problem}, and it is precisely the layer on which commercial token pricing is most punishing. The other six sevenths are simulation, non-LLM training and data processing: also memory-hungry, but in a different currency, with different access patterns and a separate accounting in BGPU$_{\mathrm{BW}}$. Both layers point at the capacity-first architecture of Chapter~\ref{ch:compute}, and they point at it for different reasons; collapsing them into one sentence about ``the national workload'' is the category error this chapter spends its length avoiding.

\input{figs/fig-ai4s-plots}

\textbf{The execution layer.} Defined function-first and estimated operator-first:
\[
G_{\mathrm{sci}} = \underbrace{G^{\mathrm{AI}}_{\mathrm{sim}}}_{\text{simulation}} + \underbrace{G_{\mathrm{tra}}}_{\text{training}} + \underbrace{G_{\mathrm{dat}}}_{\text{data processing}},
\qquad
W_{\mathrm{ag}} = N\cdot f_{\mathrm{heavy}}\cdot d_{\mathrm{duty}}\cdot w_{\mathrm{heavy}}\cdot 365 ,
\]
with $G^{\mathrm{AI}}_{\mathrm{sim}} = (\chi_{\mathrm{sim}}W_{\mathrm{ag}} + n_{\mathrm{prog}}w_{\mathrm{prog}})/H_{\mathrm{yr}}$, $G_{\mathrm{tra}} = (1-\chi_{\mathrm{sim}})W_{\mathrm{ag}}/H_{\mathrm{yr}}$, and $G_{\mathrm{dat}} = F_{\mathrm{eq}}\,g_{\mathrm{fac}}$.

Every symbol, in order. $W_{\mathrm{ag}}$ is the annual agentic-execution workload in \emph{delivered BGPU-hours}: the heavy-user fraction $f_{\mathrm{heavy}}$ (default 0.125) times the activity-by-discipline duty product $d_{\mathrm{duty}}$ (0.31---the share of days a heavy user is actually active, folded with the share of disciplines that draw on this workload) times $w_{\mathrm{heavy}}$, the measured per-heavy-user daily driver (443 delivered BGPU-hours per \emph{active heavy-user day}, on the provenance set out in Section~\ref{sec:measgap}), times 365 days, times the population $N$. Dividing BGPU-hours by $H_{\mathrm{yr}}$---the 7,884 effective hours one installed BGPU delivers per year---converts a flow of work into a stock of machines, which is why $H_{\mathrm{yr}}$ appears in the denominator of the first two terms. The superscript AI on $G^{\mathrm{AI}}_{\mathrm{sim}}$ marks AI-driven simulation specifically, distinguishing it from the classical modelling-and-simulation demand that is accounted separately in BGPU$_{\mathrm{BW}}$ and never summed with it. $\chi_{\mathrm{sim}} \in [0,1]$ is the \emph{simulation share}: the fraction of agentic execution that is surrogate-and-simulation work rather than model training, so $\chi_{\mathrm{sim}}$ and $(1-\chi_{\mathrm{sim}})$ split $W_{\mathrm{ag}}$ between the first two terms. $n_{\mathrm{prog}}$ is the census of persistent-surrogate programs and $w_{\mathrm{prog}}$ their annual cost, 0.1--0.5M BGPU-hours each. $F_{\mathrm{eq}}$ is the national inventory of large-instrument facility equivalents and $g_{\mathrm{fac}}$ the installed BGPU each requires for its data-processing pipeline, 2,400--3,250 apiece~\cite{ai4s-framework}. An explicit substrate-eligibility matrix governs which function may run on which substrate, and it is stated \emph{capability-first} rather than by product category. A substrate is eligible for training if it delivers the required low-precision arithmetic throughput, the memory capacity per device to hold weights and optimizer state under the chosen parallelism plan, the collective-injection bandwidth that plan needs, and a training software stack with demonstrated convergence at scale. Discrete accelerators presently satisfy all four; the integrated processors of Section~\ref{sec:lineshine} satisfy the first two by construction and are unproven on the last two, which is exactly what the proof suite of Section~\ref{sec:proofsuite} is designed to settle. Writing the matrix as ``training requires GPU-class systems'' would encode a vendor category into a vendor-neutral unit and would silently contradict this book's own integrated-processor argument; writing it as four measurable conditions leaves the question open to evidence, which is where it belongs. Simulation remains the most freely routable function because its eligibility conditions are the weakest---which is where a country's classical fleet can legitimately offset AI demand, and, by the same test, where it cannot.

\subsection{The serving-generation scenario: what happens when the token gets cheap}
\label{sec:serving-scenario}

Everything above prices the token layer on one canonical serving configuration. A framework that stopped there would be selling a snapshot as a physical constant, and the framework does not: its September 2026 technology-scenario section prices the configuration's obsolescence, and the result is large enough to change what a middle power should procure~\cite{ai4s-framework} [F/S].

The scenario is a \emph{serving generation} of the kind now shipping---a DeepSeek-V4.1-Flash-class combination of aggressive sparsity, cache-resident long context and a per-token key--value footprint small enough that a million-token master context stops costing what it costs today. The advance is attributed where it belongs: the published work driving it is Chinese, and the commercial expression of it is a cached-input price roughly a seventh of the cheapest Western equivalent (Section~\ref{sec:serving-collapse}). Holding the demand model otherwise fixed---same population, same tiers, same duty factors, same token rates---and cutting the serving coefficient $k$ by \textbf{3.25$\times$}:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item Japan's token-service layer falls from \textbf{72.7k to 22.4k BGPU};
  \item the national requirement falls only from \textbf{497k to 446k BGPU}, about 10\%, because simulation, non-LLM training and instrument data processing are untouched by a serving advance and they are the other six sevenths;
  \item so the token layer's share of Regime~S national demand falls from \textbf{14.3--14.6\% to $\approx$5.0\%}---from a seventh to a twentieth.
\end{itemize}

The honest statement of what this does to the chapter's own headline is the framework's own: \emph{the ``88.7\% master term'' is a property of a serving generation, not a property of agentic science}. It is what one master/sub-agent configuration, at one key--value cost, does to one year's token layer. Read as a structural constant it is wrong. Read as a priced scenario with an expiry date and a named successor it is exactly what the \textbf{[F]} future-scenario modifier of the parameter taxonomy exists for, which is why both the 14.3--14.6\% figure and the 5.0\% figure are carried forward together rather than one replacing the other.

Two consequences point in opposite directions and both are printed. \emph{For the procurement argument this strengthens the case rather than weakening it}, which is why it belongs in the main text: the commodity layer a middle power can simply buy shrinks from a seventh of its national scientific compute to a twentieth, while the layer nobody is organized to sell---the integrated execution substrate of Section~\ref{sec:ai4si-stack}---rises from six sevenths to nineteen twentieths of the requirement. A programme that had sized itself around the token layer has been sizing around the part of the problem that is deflating fastest, and the part that is not deflating is the part it would have to build. \emph{Against it}, the Jevons caution of the next section applies with full force: a token 3.25 times cheaper is an invitation to run more agents, at longer context, over deeper search trees, and the scenario holds behaviour fixed by construction. Arithmetic cannot settle whether the token layer shrinks or merely becomes better value.

Three measurement obligations attach, and the scenario should not be quoted without them. \emph{First}, the 3.25$\times$ coefficient is a serving-benchmark claim and must be re-derived under the matched-condition protocol of Section~\ref{sec:archtheory} on the national reference workload---million-token master context, 128K sub-agent context, the actual tier mix---rather than taken from a vendor throughput page or from a short-context benchmark, where sparsity flatters most and transfers least. \emph{Second}, the scenario holds $T_{\mathrm{nat}}$ fixed; the demand probe that produced $w_{\mathrm{heavy}}$ must be re-run on the new serving generation to find out whether the sustained rates $r_i$ and duty factors $u_i$ move, and in which direction. \emph{Third}, the master/sub-agent split is a decision variable and not an observation: if long context becomes cheap the rational orchestration pattern changes, so the convention that 10\% of national tokens go to the master has to be re-measured rather than carried forward. Until those three are done, 14.3--14.6\% and 5.0\% are two labelled scenarios over the same measured demand, differing only in an assumed serving coefficient, and a government quoting either without the label has converted an assumption into a fact.

\section{Typed parameters and transfer rules}

The framework's portable core is not the arithmetic but the \emph{parameter taxonomy}, and it is the closest methodological cousin this book has found to its own evidence grading. (One notational warning before the classes are named: this taxonomy's labels are \emph{local to this chapter} and are not the book's evidence grades of Section~\ref{sec:evidence}. In particular \textbf{[M]} here means ``must-measure,'' not ``author-modeled,'' and \textbf{[P]} here means ``policy choice,'' not ``primary-source claim.'' The two systems answer different questions---the global grades ask how well a claim is evidenced, the parameter classes ask whether a number may be carried across a border---and where both appear on a page, the parameter classes are the ones attached to model \emph{inputs}.) Every input is classed \textbf{[U]} universal (serving physics and benchmark calibrations that transfer across borders), \textbf{[M]} must-measure (populations, facility inventories, electricity tariffs---country-specific by construction), \textbf{[T]} transferred (behavioral quantities measured so far only in one country, carried elsewhere as labeled scenario values until replaced by a domestic probe), or \textbf{[P]} policy (allocation choices, which are decisions rather than measurements), with an orthogonal \textbf{[F]} future-scenario modifier for forward technology assumptions~\cite{ai4s-framework}. The taxonomy states which numbers may cross a border, which must never, and which may travel only with a warning label and an expiry date.

That last category is where intellectual honesty lives. The serving physics---memory-bandwidth-limited decode of trillion-parameter MoE models, calibrated delivered efficiency, generation conversion factors---transfers exactly, because benchmarks do. What a heavy national research cohort actually does under scarcity transfers only as a hypothesis: Singaporean or American researchers may behave like the measured Japanese cohort, but no one has yet checked. A portable framework must therefore do more than parameterize the arithmetic; it must \emph{label every input by what would make it trustworthy, and specify the experiment that replaces each assumption with a local measurement}. The seven-step measurement protocol does exactly that---population construction, facility inventory, a saturating demand probe on a $\sim$1,000-GPU current-generation partition with full accounting, serving-basis calibration, classical-mission accounting, cost localization, and gated publication---so that ``run this analysis for country~X'' becomes an executable checklist rather than a research project.

\section{What three countries find}

The framework has been instantiated at both extremes of national scale (Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-plots}). \emph{Japan}, the fully worked measurement-anchored example: $N\approx 500{,}000$ open-tier researchers, central 2030 requirement 497--510k BGPU. \emph{Singapore}, a pre-measurement scenario: $N\approx 57$k researchers, 55--59k BGPU---the entire national requirement smaller than Japan's proposed first-stage procurement, with the existing $\sim$1,900-GPU national research fleet at 1.5--2\% workload coverage and a proposed 3,000-GPU Rubin-class first stage reaching 14--16\% coverage and $\sim$64\% population reach for a four-year subtotal of \$0.39--0.44B. \emph{United States}: $N\approx 1.1$M, 1.09--1.17M BGPU central, with the committed Equinox-plus-Solstice systems---the largest AI procurement in DOE history---standing at $\sim$10\% workload coverage of the modeled requirement, closely paralleling Japan's no-new-procurement endpoint~\cite{ai4s-framework}.

\emph{Three national cases, not one number.} The Japanese instantiation is carried as a low--central--high triple, and the band is set by two parameters rather than by the arithmetic. \emph{Central}, 497--510k BGPU: the measured probe values on the current serving generation, the published facility inventory, the 10/20/70 entitlement profile. \emph{Low}, $\approx$300k BGPU: the central case less the $\pm$40\% behavioural band that this section argues is the minimum defensible confidence interval on the per-researcher core before a second national probe---and, independently, the serving-generation scenario of Section~\ref{sec:serving-scenario} lands at 446k, inside that band and above its floor, which is itself informative about how little a serving advance moves a requirement dominated by simulation. \emph{High}, $\approx$710k BGPU: the central case plus the same band, \emph{before} any allowance for induced demand. Both endpoints are derived from published quantities rather than asserted, and the triple is printed because a single central figure invites a reader to treat a $\pm$40\% behavioural uncertainty as a rounding error.

\emph{Four estimands, kept apart.} Conflating any two of them is the commonest failure in national compute planning, and all four appear in this chapter. \emph{Observed use} is what a metered fleet delivered: 443 BGPU-hours per active heavy-user day is this class, and it is the only measured quantity in the chain. \emph{Rationed demand} is what users would have submitted had capacity existed; the EA1 partition saturated in five weeks, so observed use is a floor on rationed demand and gives no estimate of the gap. \emph{Policy targets} are entitlements a government chooses---the 10/20/70 tier profile and the open-tier population definition are both decisions, both graded [P] in the parameter taxonomy, and neither is discovered by measurement. \emph{Forecast adoption} is the assumption that the 2030 population behaves like the measured 2026 cohort, which is the largest unverified step in the chain and the one a second probe would attack first. A national requirement is therefore a \emph{policy target evaluated under forecast adoption, anchored at one point by observed use, with rationed demand telling us the anchor is low}. It is not a measurement of 2030, and nothing in this chapter should be read as one.

Four disciplines govern how those numbers should be read, and the third and fourth are corrections to the loose way such figures are ordinarily handled. \emph{Installed peak, deliverable annual service, SLO-provisioned capacity, and effective utilization are four different quantities}, and a comparison that mixes them is meaningless; this chapter quotes the analytical design point and the operationally provisioned point separately wherever both exist. \emph{Estimands must match}: delivered usage $\ne$ requested work $\ne$ latent demand $\ne$ installed capacity $\ne$ population reach $\ne$ workload coverage. \emph{The scale-invariance finding is a hypothesis, not a result}---it holds across three instantiations of which only one is measured, so the honest statement is that the per-researcher core is \emph{modelled} as scale-invariant because both of its terms scale linearly in $N$ at fixed behaviour, and testing that assumption outside Japan is the first job of any second national probe. And \emph{the Singapore and US figures should not be quoted with more precision than transferred behavioural assumptions permit}: a reasonable confidence band on the $\approx$0.94 BGPU-per-researcher core is at least $\pm$40\% before national measurement, which is why this chapter's strategic conclusion is stated as an order-of-magnitude gap rather than as a percentage.

One further sensitivity deserves its own line, because it cuts against the chapter's own conclusion. \emph{Cheaper models may not reduce BGPU demand at all.} If capability per token rises and price per token falls, the rational institutional response is to run more agents, longer contexts, and deeper search---the Jevons argument of Section~\ref{sec:jevons} applied to science. Whether efficiency reduces or induces national compute demand is an empirical question that the measurement protocol can answer and has not yet; both signs are consistent with the framework, and this book's own bet, given the agentic direction of scientific workflows, is on induction rather than reduction. The serving-generation scenario of Section~\ref{sec:serving-scenario} is the sharpest instance: it assumes fixed behaviour by construction and therefore reports the \emph{reduction} branch in full, which is why it is labelled [F/S] and carried beside the current-generation figures rather than replacing them. A reader who believes the induction branch should read the 5.0\% token share as a floor on that share and the 446k requirement as a floor on that requirement.

Three structural findings survive across the $\sim$20$\times$ range of national scale. \emph{The per-researcher core is modelled as scale-invariant}: the token layer plus the agentic execution term compose to $\approx$0.94 BGPU per open-tier researcher in every country, because both scale linearly in $N$ at fixed behavior. \emph{The deviations are diagnostic}: what does not scale with $N$ is the facility-and-program term, set by instrument bandwidth and pipeline depth---$\sim$5.5\% of the total for Singapore, $\sim$9\% for the United States, where the streaming-inference class alone runs 60--132k BGPU, between half and 1.2 times the entire committed AI fleet. Sizing exercises that count only central systems will structurally undercount this class. \emph{And the headcount rule is worth a factor of six}: an OECD-consistent FTE count of US open-sector researchers is $\approx$185k against an eligibility headcount of $\approx$1.1M---a definitional choice that swamps every other sensitivity, and therefore one that any official exercise must state, defend, and apply consistently.

\section{The universal-reach ladder, and the order-of-magnitude gap}

The framework's sharpest instrument is a computed \emph{universal-reach ladder}: the installed capacity at which 100\% of researchers are served, at each of two precisely labeled service rungs. Rung one, \emph{uniform baseline service}---every researcher receiving the baseline 150\,Mtok/month tier---costs 3.2k (Singapore) / 28.3k (Japan) / 62.3k (US) BGPU installed. Rung two, \emph{full-population tiered-token service} at the differentiated 10/20/70 entitlement profile, costs 8.3k / 72.7k / 160.0k~\cite{ai4s-framework}.

The distinction sharpens both readings, and Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-plots} (middle) shows why. Japan's and the United States' committed fleets already \emph{exceed} the uniform-baseline rung---rung coverage 137\% and 180\% at the annual-average convention, 106\% and 139\% even at SLO provisioning---yet stand at only $\sim$54\% and $\sim$70\% of the tiered rung, while Singapore's existing fleet covers under a third of even the baseline. Closing the US tiered-service gap takes roughly 19,000 Rubin-class GPUs beyond current commitments. And the full requirement---tiered tokens plus full heavy compute---costs a further $\sim$6$\times$ again: 54k / 470k / 1,034k BGPU. Those three figures are the \emph{per-researcher core} scaled to full population and exclude the facility-and-program term, which is why Japan's 470k sits below the 497--510k national requirement quoted above; the difference is the instrument-pipeline demand that does not scale with headcount, and it is stated separately precisely because sizing exercises that count only central systems omit it.

Stated per researcher (Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-plots}, bottom), the result is the chapter's headline: \textbf{announced national infrastructures provision 0.02--0.10 BGPU per open-tier researcher against a modeled requirement near 1.0}---an underprovision of roughly tenfold for the United States and for Japan before its proposed first stage, and closer to fiftyfold for Singapore. One more asymmetry is worth recording because it is the reason this chapter exists: on the public record, Japan's stage-1 proposal is sized against a measured national demand model but is unfunded, while the US commitment is funded, contracted, and the largest in the world but is not yet sized against its research population. \emph{One side has the measurement without the money; the other has the money without the measurement.}

\section{What this means for the argument of this book}

Four consequences, each connecting to a thread already running.

\emph{The token price is the science budget---at a stated premium, for a stated layer.} Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} derives, from a token-layer workload share of $f = 0.146$ and two assumed purchased-token premiums, that buying that layer entirely at a premium consumes 77.4\% ($\rho = 20$) or 91.1\% ($\rho = 60$) of a Regime~S budget (Figure~\ref{fig:ai4s-cost}). This chapter supplies the mechanism and, with it, the denominators that keep the claim honest: the master term is 88.7\% \emph{of the token-service layer}, the token-service layer is 14.3--14.6\% of national demand, and therefore the master term is about 13.0\% of national AI4S compute. It is nonetheless the expensive 13\%, because it is exactly the long-context orchestration that no agentic workflow can avoid and that commercial pricing charges most for.

The defensible statement is thus a \emph{budget} statement at an assumed premium, not a \emph{capacity} statement. A nation that rents its master layer at a 20--60$\times$ premium has rented about a seventh of its research computing and paid three quarters to nine tenths of its AI4S bill for it; if it routes selectively, escalating only the tenth of the token layer that genuinely needs the frontier tier, the same layer costs 33.1\% or 54.1\% of the budget instead; and on the serving-generation scenario of Section~\ref{sec:serving-scenario} the four figures fall to 48.5\%, 73.8\%, 12.0\% and 24.5\%---evaluated at $f_{\mathrm{front}} = 0.045$, the conservative end of the 4.5--5.0\% band the 3.25$\times$ cut produces, the two ends differing only in whether the shrunken token layer is taken against the pre-scenario requirement of 497k BGPU or the post-scenario 446k; at the 5.0\% end they are 51.3\%, 75.9\%, 13.2\% and 26.6\%. Renting the master layer is not renting the majority of research computing. It is paying the majority of the bill for a seventh of it, at a premium the buyer does not set---and the distance between those two sentences is the distance between a capacity argument and a price argument.

\emph{The token layer is a memory problem, and therefore an architecture problem.} If the token-service layer is dominated by million-token-context decode over trillion-parameter sparse models---and the execution layer, separately, by bandwidth-bound simulation and instrument-rate data processing---then the machine that serves both best is capacity-rich and bandwidth-balanced---the design point of Section~\ref{sec:lpddr6} and the LX2 class of Section~\ref{sec:lineshine}, not a dense-FLOP tensor part. The sizing framework and the architecture argument are the same argument in different units.

\emph{Sovereignty has a computable price, and it is high but not absurd.} At delivered B200-hour costs of \$1.84--2.19 across the three countries~\cite{ai4s-framework}, the gap between announced and required capacity is a national-budget question rather than a technological impossibility---which is exactly the condition under which pooling becomes rational. What pooling does, and what it cannot do, has to be stated arithmetically, because the loose version of the claim does not survive contact with a conservation law. \emph{Federation does not create compute-hours.} Three fleets each roughly tenfold short of their own national requirement, pooled, are one fleet roughly tenfold short of the sum of those requirements. No scheduling arrangement changes the numerator.

Four quantities have to be distinguished, because the entire case for federation lives in the differences between them. \emph{Uptime} is the fraction of wall-clock time a device is healthy. \emph{Occupancy} is the fraction of healthy time it is assigned to a job. \emph{Effective utilization} is the fraction of wall-clock time it does useful work---roughly the product of the first two, net of failed and restarted work, and it is the quantity the framework's $\alpha = 0.90$ denotes. \emph{Rationed demand} is the work users would have submitted had capacity existed, which the EA1 saturation shows exceeds delivered work by an unmeasured margin. The arithmetic is then unforgiving: moving effective utilization from 0.90 to a theoretical 1.00 gains \textbf{at most 11.1\%} of delivered hours, and no real federation reaches 1.00. That is a worthwhile gain---roughly a year of fleet growth obtained without a procurement---and it is one order of magnitude short of the gap.

What federation actually supplies is therefore four things, none of which is aggregate capacity. \emph{Access}: a researcher in a member country reaches a class of machine their own country does not operate, which changes what can be attempted even when total hours are unchanged. \emph{Peak sharing}: campaign cycles, conference deadlines and instrument runs are not synchronized across time zones, so a pool's peak-to-mean ratio is lower than any member's and the same installed base absorbs more peaks without queueing them. \emph{Reduced duplication}: one certified data pipeline, one evaluation suite, one trust stack, built once instead of five times---a saving in effort and calendar rather than in GPU-hours, and on the evidence of Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} the larger of the two. \emph{Utilization}: the bounded gain above. A federation that promises the missing order of magnitude is promising something conservation forbids, and will be measured against it. A federation that promises access, peak sharing, shared build cost and a utilization gain of at most about a tenth is promising things it can deliver---and that, stated at its true size, is the operational case for Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium}, which now has a denominator.

\emph{And comparability is itself strategic.} A country that cannot state its requirement in a unit others recognize cannot join a federation, cannot audit a vendor's claim, and cannot tell whether a foreign price list is cheap or ruinous. The framework's real product is not the Japanese number; it is the shared definition---which is why the invitation it extends, to run the protocol and publish the parameter file, is the least expensive strategic action available to any government reading this book.
