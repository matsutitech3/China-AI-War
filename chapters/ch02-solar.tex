\chapter{Solar Photovoltaics: The Template}
\label{ch:solar}

Solar PV is the template war: the first, the most complete, and the one whose mechanics were most visibly repeated in batteries and EVs. Every structural element of the playbook appears here in its purest form, and the final state---above 90\% Chinese share at every stage of the supply chain---defines what ``winning'' means in this book.

\section{The West invents, the West leads (1954--2007)}

The silicon solar cell was invented at Bell Labs in 1954; the modern PV industry was built on American, German, Japanese, and Australian science. As late as 2006, Sharp of Japan was the world's largest cell producer; in 2007 Germany's Q-Cells overtook it at 370\,MW of annual production~\cite{qcells-sharp}. Demand was created almost entirely by Western policy: Germany's 2004 EEG amendment set rooftop feed-in tariffs at 57.4 euro cents per kWh, guaranteed for twenty years, igniting the first global solar boom~\cite{german-fit}. The scientific pipeline that would matter most, however, ran through Australia: Shi Zhengrong, a PhD student of Martin Green at UNSW, returned to China to found Suntech Power in Wuxi in 2001---with municipal-government seed capital.

\section{The first scale-up and the first bankruptcies (2005--2013)}

Suntech listed on the NYSE in December 2005---the first private Chinese company to do so---and Shi briefly became the richest man in China; by end-2011 Suntech's capacity had reached 2\,GW~\cite{suntech}. Chinese producers, feeding on German and later Italian and Spanish FiT demand, scaled cell and module manufacturing at a pace no Western firm attempted. The financing was policy: in 2010 alone, the China Development Bank extended approximately \$47 billion in credit lines to renewable-energy manufacturers, including \$7.8\,billion to Suntech, \$5.6\,billion to Yingli, and \$4.7\,billion each to Trina and JA Solar~\cite{cdb-credit}. In October 2010 the State Council designated new energy one of seven Strategic Emerging Industries, and the 12th Five-Year Plan for Solar PV set explicit industrial targets~\cite{sei-solar}.

The polysilicon price collapse did the rest. Spot polysilicon peaked near \$475/kg in March 2008; by December 2012 it was below \$16/kg~\cite{bernreuter}. Western and Japanese incumbents, structured for high-price niche markets, were annihilated by the combination of Chinese capacity and collapsing input costs: Solyndra failed in 2011 despite a \$535 million US loan guarantee; Q-Cells---world number one four years earlier---entered insolvency in April 2012 and was sold to Korea's Hanwha; SolarWorld, the petitioner of the US trade cases, followed in 2017~\cite{solyndra,qcells-insolvency,solarworld}.

Notably, the shakeout consumed the Chinese pioneers too. In March 2013 Suntech defaulted on \$541 million of convertible bonds---the first mainland Chinese default on US bonds---and its Wuxi subsidiary entered bankruptcy~\cite{suntech}. This is a recurring and under-appreciated feature of the playbook: China does not protect its champions individually; it protects the \emph{industry}, and lets domestic competition kill firms freely. The capacity, the workforce, and the supplier network survive the corpses.

\section{Trade defenses fail (2012--2018)}

The Western response was tariffs. The US Commerce Department's October 2012 determinations set anti-dumping margins of 18--32\% on major Chinese producers (250\% country-wide) plus countervailing duties of ~15\%~\cite{us-adcvd}. The EU imposed provisional duties in June 2013, scheduled to average 47.6\%, but---under Chinese pressure on other trade fronts---settled within weeks for a minimum-import-price undertaking~\cite{eu-solar}. The 2018 US Section~201 safeguard added a 30\% tariff~\cite{s201}.

None of it worked. Chinese producers moved cell and module assembly to Southeast Asia, moved up the value chain, and---decisively---kept the learning curve. Tariffs taxed Western deployment without resurrecting Western manufacturing; by the time the tariff walls were fully built, there was no meaningful Western industry left inside them to protect.

\section{Domestic demand as strategic ballast (2011--2026)}

From 2011 China built a domestic FiT-driven market that eventually dwarfed the export markets, insulating its manufacturers from Western trade policy entirely. The ``531'' shock of May 2018---an overnight cut of FiTs and subsidized quotas---was itself a deliberate stress test that culled weaker producers~\cite{policy531}. The Top Runner auctions (2015--2017) tied market access to efficiency floors, forcing PERC and mono-crystalline technology from niche to standard~\cite{toprunner}. Domestic installations reached 277\,GW$_{\mathrm{AC}}$ in 2024 and 315\,GW$_{\mathrm{AC}}$ in 2025 (roughly 415\,GW$_{\mathrm{DC}}$, about 60\% of world additions), bringing cumulative Chinese capacity to 1.2\,TW---by itself more than the entire world had installed as of 2022~\cite{china-2024-installs,china-2025-installs}.

\section{The end state: total supply-chain capture}

Table~\ref{tab:solar-shares} shows the 2023 production shares by stage. The IEA's assessment is that shares of polysilicon, ingot, and wafer capacity are converging on 95\%; nine of the top ten polysilicon producers are Chinese (Germany's Wacker, the survivor, fell to eighth); and Chinese manufacturing costs run 10\% below India, 20\% below the US, and 35\% below Europe~\cite{iea-supply,poly-2024}. Global module capacity reached $\sim$1.8\,TW/yr in 2025 against $\sim$0.6--0.7\,TW of demand~\cite{module-capacity}; China exported 268\,GW of modules in 2025, and cell exports grew 73\% as tariff-protected markets imported the layer below the tariff line instead~\cite{infolink-2025,ember-cells}.

The cost curve is the weapon. Across five decades the module price fell from \$106 per watt to roughly nine cents---a 99.9\% decline---following a Wright's-law learning rate of about 20\% per doubling of cumulative production~\cite{owid-learning,energytrend-prices}. Whoever manufactures the doublings owns the learning; from roughly 2010 onward, essentially all doublings were Chinese. \section{Winning is expensive: the involution phase}

Victory did not end the war economics. With capacity at triple demand, 2024--2025 prices fell below cash cost across the chain: polysilicon dipped under \$4.50/kg, and five Chinese majors forecast combined 2025 net losses of RMB 29--33 billion (\$4.1--4.7\,billion)~\cite{pv-losses-2025}. Beijing's response is instructive for the AI chapters: in July 2025 Xi Jinping personally launched the ``anti-involution'' campaign against ``disorderly low-price competition''; the six largest polysilicon producers organized a $\sim$RMB 50 billion fund to buy out and retire a third of national capacity; and in December 2025 an official ``storage and integration platform'' was chartered to manage polysilicon supply---an OPEC-for-solar~\cite{anti-involution,poly-fund,poly-platform}. Having captured the industry, the state now moves to cartelize it and harvest stable rents. Meanwhile technology leadership has also passed to China: LONGi holds the world perovskite-tandem efficiency record (35.5\%, July 2026), and Trina leads global perovskite patent filings~\cite{longi-record,trina-patents}.

\section{The Western rebuild that wasn't}

The US Inflation Reduction Act briefly built module-assembly capacity from 8\,GW to 56.5\,GW (mid-2025), but cell capacity barely materialized, and the July 2025 OBBBA phase-out of deployment credits cut ten-year US installation forecasts by 17--41\%~\cite{ira-build,obbba}. First Solar survives---profitably---as a CdTe niche protected by tariffs and its non-silicon technology; it produced 16.1\,GW in 2025, roughly \emph{one fortieth} of Chinese module shipments~\cite{firstsolar}. That ratio, four decades after the US invented the technology, is the terminal scoreboard of the first war.

\section{Lessons carried forward}

Four lessons from solar recur throughout this book. \emph{First}, trade barriers imposed after cost-curve capture protect nothing but domestic prices. \emph{Second}, the champions are disposable; the industrial commons is not. \emph{Third}, a subsidized home market large enough to absorb half of world demand makes foreign trade policy irrelevant. \emph{Fourth}, the endgame of commoditization is state-managed consolidation---dominance first, margins later. Keep all four in mind when reading about open-weight models priced near zero.
