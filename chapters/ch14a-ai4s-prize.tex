\chapter{The Largest Prize: Why AI for Science Dominates the Value Case}
\chaptermark{The Largest Prize}
\label{ch:prize}

This book has so far treated AI as an industrial commodity and asked who will supply it. This chapter asks what it is \emph{for}, and argues against the ordering most of the public debate assumes, in which writing software and streamlining enterprise workflow are where the value is and science is a research interest.

The claim actually defended here is narrower than this chapter's title, and the difference is stated at the outset rather than left for a careful reader to find. What the evidence supports is that \emph{AI applied to science is a major pathway to value, with unusually large spillovers, an unusually large base, and an unusually strong case for public rather than private provision}. What the evidence does not support---and what this chapter therefore does not assert---is a \emph{ranking} of that pathway above enterprise or software applications on a common accounting. Section~\ref{sec:denominator} sets out precisely why such a ranking cannot be established from the figures available, and the argument is built so that nothing downstream depends on it: the case for public investment rests on spillovers, coordination failure, access, and scientific priority-setting, each of which stands whether science is the largest prize or one of the large ones.

Read with that qualification, the two chapters that follow---how much compute a nation's research actually needs, and what institution could supply it---are not a digression from this book's argument but its destination, and the competition described in Parts~I--III is in substantial part a competition over who gets to compound their scientific output fastest.

The chapter proceeds in the order that honesty requires: first the demonstrated results, then an unsparing ledger of what has \emph{not} worked, then the economics that make the prize large even after the failures are subtracted, then the strategic consequence.

\section{What has actually been demonstrated}

Four domains have produced results that survive scrutiny, and their common feature is instructive: in each, AI replaced a computation that was previously bounded by physics-based simulation cost, and the replacement was validated against an established operational baseline rather than against a benchmark of its own devising.

\textbf{Weather is the strongest case, and the only one with operational adoption by three national agencies.} ECMWF's AIFS became operational in February 2025 at 28\,km grid spacing with gains up to 20\% over the physics-based model on measures including tropical-cyclone tracks, at roughly \emph{one-thousandth} of the energy per forecast; its 51-member ensemble followed in July 2025~\cite{ecmwf-aifs}. NOAA deployed AI-driven global models operationally in December 2025: a 16-day forecast in about 40 minutes using \emph{0.3\%} of the compute of its conventional system, with the ensemble variant extending useful skill by 18--24 hours~\cite{noaa-ai}. Microsoft's Aurora---1.3B parameters, pretrained on 32 GPUs in about two and a half weeks---beats the operational high-resolution model on 92\% of targets and matched or beat all seven operational centres on every tested cyclone track, at roughly $10^5$ times the speed of the atmospheric-chemistry system it replaces; adapting it to a new task takes four to eight weeks against the several years a new dynamical model requires~\cite{aurora}. NeuralGCM runs on a single accelerator what previously required 13,000 CPUs~\cite{neuralgcm}. China's meteorological administration has its own operational family, and a 2026 typhoon-track system reports up to 32\% error reduction against leading global ensembles~\cite{cma-ai,fuxi-cnops}.

\textbf{Structural biology is the most diffused case.} AlphaFold has predicted over 200 million structures, is used by more than three million researchers in 190 countries---including over a million in low- and middle-income countries---and won the 2024 Nobel Prize in Chemistry~\cite{alphafold-impact,nobel-2024}. The measurable second-order effect matters more than the structures: researchers using it increased their submissions of \emph{novel experimental} structures by over 40\% and were twice as likely to be cited in clinical work~\cite{alphafold-impact}. The database now includes millions of predicted complexes, representing on the order of 17 million GPU-hours of computation given away~\cite{alphafold-db}. Protein \emph{design} has followed: a generative model produced a fluorescent protein 58\% identical to its nearest natural relative---an object evolution had not found---and diffusion-based enzyme design has produced metallohydrolases with catalytic efficiency orders of magnitude above previous designed enzymes~\cite{esm3,rfdiffusion3}.

\textbf{Mathematics and algorithm discovery produced the first credible autonomous results.} A frontier system achieved gold-medal standard at the 2025 International Mathematical Olympiad, solving five of six problems end-to-end in natural language within the human time limit~\cite{imo-gold}. More consequentially for this book, an evolutionary coding agent found a 48-multiplication algorithm for $4\times4$ complex matrix multiplication---the first improvement on that case since Strassen in 1969---improved the best known solution on 20\% of fifty-plus open problems it was set, and returned measurable production value: 0.7\% of a hyperscaler's worldwide compute recovered through better scheduling, a 23\% kernel speedup yielding a 1\% reduction in the training time of the company's own frontier model~\cite{alphaevolve}. That last result is the flywheel of Section~\ref{sec:flywheel} in miniature: AI improving the infrastructure that trains AI.

\textbf{Drug discovery has its first controlled clinical datapoint.} An AI-discovered TNIK inhibitor for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis reported Phase~IIa results in \emph{Nature Medicine} in June 2025---71 patients, 12 weeks, a $+98.4$\,mL mean forced-vital-capacity change against $-20.3$\,mL for placebo---and entered a 320-patient Phase~III in July 2026, whose first patient was dosed on 10 September at one of 47 Chinese centres, with annual forced-vital-capacity decline over 52 weeks as the primary endpoint~\cite{sci-insilico-dosing} [P]; the developer reports reaching preclinical candidate nomination in 12--18 months with 60--200 molecules synthesized per programme, against a conventional 2.5--4 years~\cite{rentosertib,rentosertib-p3}. This is one drug, one surrogate endpoint, one country, and not an approval. It is also more than the field had two years ago.

\textbf{Mathematics crossed a threshold in August 2026.} OpenAI reported solutions to ten long-standing open problems in mathematics and quantum complexity theory for roughly \$2{,}000 of inference compute, and---the part that matters for this book's epistemics---published the proofs for independent verification rather than the claim alone~\cite{astra2026} [P]. The attribution needs care. The August announcement as recorded in~\cite{astra2026} names the Astra model, but nothing else in the public record connects the released GPT-6 Astra product to the mathematics results, and for the September and October results OpenAI describes the system only as an ``unreleased internal model''~\cite{sci-rw-openai} [A]. This book therefore attributes the August results to OpenAI on the company's own account, attributes the October results to an OpenAI model the company has not named, and rests no argument on the name. As of \datafreeze the ten August proofs remain [P]: under community verification, without the independent confirmation that would move them to [V]. Two readings coexist. As AI4S evidence it is the strongest single capability signal of the summer in the domain where validation is cheapest---a formal proof either checks or it does not, although whether the checked statement is the theorem claimed is a separate question (Section~\ref{sec:sci-october-release})---which is why mathematics leads the funnel's conversion ordering. As competitive evidence it cuts against any complacent reading of this book's thesis: the recursive loop of Section~\ref{sec:rsi-branch} runs through exactly this kind of result, and it happened on closed American compute, not on the commons. What happened when the same laboratory released its output at volume in October is the first full-scale test of the funnel of Section~\ref{sec:funnel}, and it is examined there.

\section{The failure ledger}

A chapter whose title puts AI4S first is obliged to lead with the evidence against itself, and in this field the counterevidence is unusually sharp.

\emph{The most-cited economic study of AI in scientific discovery was fabricated.} A 2024 paper reporting large gains in materials discovered, patents, and product innovation at a real firm was repudiated by its institution in May 2025---``no confidence in the provenance, reliability or validity of the data''---and the two prominent economists who had publicly praised it jointly stated that its findings should not be relied upon~\cite{mit-repudiation}. It is not cited here except as a warning, and any AI4S argument that still rests on it should be discarded.

\emph{Materials discovery, the flagship domain, has not survived validation intact.} The autonomous-synthesis result that anchored the field was formally corrected in January 2026---successes cut from 40 to 36, one compound removed because it was in the training data, and the authors conceding the materials were ``new to the prediction platform, not necessarily new to science''~\cite{alab-correction}. Independent crystallographers argued that none of the originally reported compounds was new~\cite{alab-critics}. The 380,000-stable-crystal result has been criticized by senior materials chemists for producing ``chemical compounds rather than materials'' with no demonstrated functionality~\cite{gnome-critique}. The strongest generative-materials paper validated exactly one compound experimentally, and it missed its target modulus by about 20\%~\cite{mattergen}.

\emph{Even weather has a documented failure mode.} In May 2026 ECMWF discontinued real-time running of all four external AI models, having found them increasingly sensitive to the exact initializing analysis---several degraded when initialized on an upgraded analysis system---and noting that two of them do not forecast precipitation at all~\cite{ecmwf-farewell}. The lesson is not that AI weather models failed; the centre's own AIFS remains operational. It is that these models are couplings to an underlying data-assimilation system, not free-standing oracles.

\emph{Autonomous discovery is far weaker than its marketing.} When a frontier agent was run against 700 open problems in one mathematical corpus, human adjudication of 200 candidate solutions found 68.5\% fundamentally flawed and only 6.5\% meaningfully correct---of which two were genuine autonomous resolutions~\cite{aletheia}. A leading mathematician's assessment of a widely publicized single success was that only 1--2\% of the open problems in that corpus are tractable this way, that difficulty within it varies by orders of magnitude, and that unreported failures skew the perceived rate~\cite{tao}. The first conference to accept AI as primary author found submissions ``technically correct'' but ``neither interesting nor important,'' with referees noting that technical fluency can mask poor scientific judgement~\cite{agents4science}. Working scientists interviewed about frontier models in early 2026 were blunt: ``We have not found, yet, that LLMs are fundamentally changing the way that science is done''~\cite{openai-science}. The largest single entry in this ledger was written in October 2026, when 722 machine-generated mathematics manuscripts met their first day of scrutiny; it is worked through the funnel in Section~\ref{sec:sci-october-release}.

\emph{And the scientific commons is absorbing real damage.} One journal measuring its own pipeline found submissions up 42\% since late 2022, over 30\% of referee reports showing detectable AI use, and papers with heavy AI content receiving revise-and-resubmit at 3.2\% against 11.9\% for low-AI papers---while the editorial board nearly doubled to cope~\cite{ai-slop}.

The summary: AI4S has delivered decisively where the task is \emph{a well-posed prediction problem with an expensive physics-based incumbent and an established validation baseline}, and has so far mostly failed where the task is \emph{judgement}---choosing which question is worth asking, deciding what counts as new, or assessing whether a result matters. That boundary is the most useful thing known about the field, and it has a direct architectural implication taken up in Section~\ref{sec:prize-arch}.

\section{Why the prize is nevertheless the largest one}

\subsection{The denominator}
\label{sec:denominator}

Start with the sizes of the things being compared. Global research and development spending ran approximately \$3.1 trillion (PPP) in 2022, with the United States at \$923B and China at \$812B~\cite{nsf-indicators}. The entire worldwide software market---the addressable universe of ``AI writes code'' and ``AI streamlines the enterprise''---is forecast at \$1.47 trillion for 2026, roughly half of global R\&D~\cite{gartner}. And the combined revenue of the frontier laboratories whose valuations Chapter~\ref{ch:trade} examined is under 2\% of annual global R\&D spending~\cite{lab-revenue}. Even the most-cited bullish estimate of generative AI's economic potential put \emph{R\&D as the single largest affected function} at about a quarter of the total, ahead of software engineering---while explicitly excluding value from entirely new product categories, which is precisely where scientific advance shows up~\cite{mckinsey-genai}.

\emph{What that comparison does not establish, stated before it is used.} It does not establish a ranking, and this chapter does not use it as one. The \$3.1T figure is global R\&D \emph{expenditure}, PPP-adjusted, for 2022; the \$1.47T figure is a forecast of worldwide software-industry \emph{revenue} for 2026, nominal, covering what vendors sell rather than what their customers gain. Different years, different price bases, different scopes, opposite sides of the ledger. A ranking would require a comparable-accounting box---one year, one price basis, one consistently chosen unit (expenditure, or value added, or social surplus), and one attribution rule for how much of an outcome is credited to the AI rather than to the researchers, capital and institutions around it---and this book cannot build one, because the input--output tables, attribution conventions and counterfactuals that a general-purpose technology would need do not exist in published form. Two further objections cut the same way. \emph{Enterprise AI's value is not confined to software vendors' revenue}: most of it accrues to the firms deploying it, as lower costs and better decisions, and never appears in a software-market total at all---so the \$1.47T denominator understates the thing it stands in for, possibly by a large factor. And \emph{R\&D spending is an input measure}, so treating it as the size of the prize assumes exactly the conversion that the funnel of Section~\ref{sec:funnel} exists to discount.

What the numbers do establish is enough for the argument that follows: the base is enormous, it is the same order of magnitude as the entire software industry on any accounting one chooses, and an independent bullish estimate places R\&D first among affected functions. The defensible statement is therefore \emph{a major pathway with substantial spillovers}---and the strategic conclusions of this chapter are derived from spillovers, coordination failure, access and priority-setting, not from a league table.

\subsection{The return}

Research spending is not an ordinary input. The best available estimate of the social return to R\&D is \$13.3 of social benefit per dollar spent at a 5\% discount rate---and even under a deliberately punitive 20-year-lag assumption, \$4.9 per dollar; the authors note it is difficult to construct an average return below \$4~\cite{jones-summers}. No enterprise-software deployment has a publicly documented multiplier in that range---though that comparison sets a well-studied literature against a thin one, which is not the same as setting two measured quantities against each other.

The usual explanation is that enterprise software reallocates surplus between firms while research generates surplus mostly not captured by whoever paid for it. That is right about the second half and too sweeping about the first, and the overreach should be corrected here rather than defended. Several enterprise categories are plainly not reallocation: software engineering, semiconductor design and industrial process optimization improve the tools with which the next generation of technology---including the next generation of AI---is built, and their gains have the same non-appropriable, tool-improving character that the R\&D return literature measures. A compiler optimization, a place-and-route improvement or a yield gain diffuses through everyone who uses the chip. What can be said with the evidence available is narrower and still sufficient: the non-appropriability of research returns is \emph{measured}, the appropriability of enterprise returns is \emph{assumed}, the assumption is wrong at the margin for exactly the tool-improving applications, and the public-provision argument does not rest on it in any case. It rests on non-appropriability where it is measured, on the coordination failure that leaves shared scientific infrastructure underbuilt, and on access---all three of which Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} builds an institution on.

Three accounting disciplines keep this subsection honest. \emph{First}, the \$4--13 figure is an \emph{intertemporal present value} of the social surplus stream generated by a dollar of R\&D, not an annual multiplier; it must never be multiplied directly against a single year's R\&D flow to produce an annual benefit. The correct chain is: an annual \emph{productivity uplift} to the research enterprise (the funnel's output, below) $\rightarrow$ an increment to the effective R\&D flow $\rightarrow$ a \emph{present value} of social surplus obtained by applying the Jones--Summers ratio to that increment. The three quantities---annual uplift, incremental output flow, and present value of surplus---are kept separate wherever this book uses them. \emph{Second}, the ratio is an \emph{average} return; AI will first reach the most computationally tractable tasks, whose marginal return may sit above or below that average, and nothing in the current evidence pins down which. \emph{Third}, the \$3.1T denominator is PPP-adjusted while software and laboratory revenues are nominal; the comparison is strategically suggestive, not an accounting identity. None of these disciplines shrinks the qualitative conclusion---the base is enormous and the multiplier is large---but they change what may legitimately be computed from it.

\subsection{The bottleneck}

Now the argument that makes AI4S strategic rather than merely valuable. Research productivity is falling. The canonical measurement finds aggregate US research productivity down by a factor of 41 since the 1930s---more than 5\% per year---sustained only by a 23-fold increase in research effort; keeping Moore's law required more than eighteen times as many researchers per doubling as in the early 1970s; cancer clinical-trial research productivity fell by a factor of 4.8 between 1975 and 2006~\cite{ideas-harder}. Ideas are getting harder to find, and the traditional remedy---hire more researchers---is exhausting itself demographically in every advanced economy.

An important 2026 counter-interpretation deserves its place here, because it redirects rather than refutes the argument. Fort and coauthors find that patent production per unit of R\&D input may \emph{not} be declining at all; what has weakened is the conversion of ideas into firm growth---the diffusion step, not the discovery step~\cite{fort2026}. The distinction matters strategically. If ideas are in fact harder to produce, AI's leverage is on discovery itself, and the funnel below is the right model. If ideas are produced but diffuse poorly, the binding constraint is institutional---standards, competition, technology transfer, absorptive capacity---and the highest-return intervention is the deployment and certification infrastructure of Chapters~\ref{ch:trust} and~\ref{ch:consortium} rather than more model capability. The truth almost certainly varies by sector, which is one more reason the consortium proposal pairs compute provision with diffusion machinery instead of treating capability as self-executing. Either way the case for the investment survives; what changes is where the marginal dollar goes.

An input that raises research productivity therefore acts on the binding constraint of long-run growth, which is why the economics of AI turns on this question and not on call-centre automation. And here is the pivot of this chapter. The most influential \emph{skeptical} estimate of AI's macroeconomic effect---a total-factor-productivity gain of about 0.66\% over ten years, roughly one-tenth of bullish estimates---reaches that number by counting only task-level automation of existing work, and \emph{explicitly excludes} transformative science-driven gains on the stated grounds that advances of the protein-folding type ``do not seem likely within the 10-year time frame''~\cite{acemoglu}. That exclusion is the entire disagreement. Structure-prediction, operational AI weather, and designed enzymes all arrived inside the window. One does not have to accept the most aggressive growth models---one recent analysis finds that automating as little as 13\% of research tasks economy-wide is sufficient to enter an explosive-growth regime~\cite{davidson}---to notice that the credible range of estimates for AI's contribution to annual growth spans from 0.1\% to 30\%, and that the single variable separating those poles is whether AI automates \emph{innovation itself}~\cite{quadrillion}.

\subsection{The flywheel}
\label{sec:flywheel}

The final reason AI4S outranks the alternatives is that it is the only application category that feeds back into the means of its own production. Better materials improve batteries, magnets, and semiconductors; better semiconductor design improves accelerators; better weather and grid modelling improves the energy system that Chapter~\ref{ch:energy} identified as the binding physical constraint; better algorithms improve training efficiency directly, as the 1\%-of-training-time result cited above demonstrates in the smallest possible way. Each of these is an input to the war this book describes. The honest boundary is narrower than ``enterprise AI has no such loop.'' A faster expense-report pipeline does not lower the cost of the next accelerator generation; AI-assisted semiconductor design, compiler and kernel work, and industrial process optimization plainly do, which is why the 1\%-of-training-time result above is classified here as a flywheel case even though it was produced inside a commercial engineering organization rather than a laboratory. The distinction that survives is between applications that improve the means of production and applications that improve an administrative process---a line that runs \emph{through} the enterprise category rather than around it, and that puts the tool-improving part of enterprise AI on the same side of the ledger as science.

\section{The value-conversion funnel}
\label{sec:funnel}

The claim that AI4S is a major pathway to value needs a conversion model, not just a denominator, because value does not pass unchanged from capability to output. The chain runs
\[
\text{AI capability} \rightarrow \text{task acceleration} \rightarrow \text{validated discovery} \rightarrow \text{deployment} \rightarrow \text{productivity},
\]
and each arrow has a conversion efficiency well below one. Stating them explicitly---even as ranges---is the difference between an argument and an enthusiasm.

\input{figs/fig-funnel}

Four disciplines govern the funnel. \emph{First, addressability}: not all of the \$3.1T R\&D denominator is reachable by current or near-term AI. The reachable share is dominated by the task classes where the evidence above is strong---prediction with an established validation baseline, and search or optimization over well-defined spaces---and is much thinner for autonomous experimental planning and thinner still for scientific judgement and theory formation. A defensible current addressable share is on the order of 15--35\% of research \emph{effort}, weighted toward computational and screening-heavy fields [M]. \emph{Second, acceleration is not discovery}: a hundred-thousand-fold speedup in a forward model, as in weather, converts into scientific value only where the bottleneck was that computation; where the bottleneck is experimental throughput, funding, or judgement, the speedup converts at a small fraction. \emph{Third, validation attrition is severe and measurable}: the materials record of the previous section is the cautionary case, where headline discovery counts survived external scrutiny at rates well below one. \emph{Fourth, deployment and regulatory lag} truncate everything: a validated drug candidate is a decade from revenue, a validated material perhaps half that, a validated forecast improvement is operational within a year---which is why weather is the domain where AI4S value is already visible in the statistics and pharmaceuticals is the domain where it is not yet.

\subsection{The arithmetic, done properly}
\label{sec:funnelmath}

It is often said that multiplying these ranges yields a 3--30\% uplift. It does not, and the error is worth walking through, because the corrected arithmetic is what the rest of this section rests on. Define the output variable precisely: the \emph{incremental} uplift to realized research output from AI, for a single homogeneous task pool, is
\begin{equation}
U = a\,(s-1)\,v\,d,
\label{eq:funnel-simple}
\end{equation}
whose four factors are the four arrows of the funnel: $a$ is \emph{addressability}, the share of research effort that current AI can reach at all; $s$ is the \emph{speed-up} on that reachable share, so that $(s-1)$ counts only the acceleration \emph{beyond} the unassisted baseline (a model that changes nothing has $s=1$ and contributes zero); $v$ is \emph{validation survival}, the fraction of accelerated output that withstands independent scrutiny; and $d$ is \emph{deployment conversion}, the fraction of validated results that actually reach use. All four are dimensionless fractions except $s$, which is a multiplier.

One statement about Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-simple} has to be made before it is used, because two different production models are routinely written with the same symbols. This is a \emph{task-output} model: it asks how much more output a fixed research effort produces when a share $a$ of its tasks runs $s$ times faster, and it is linear in $a$. It is \emph{not} the serial-time (Amdahl) model, $U_{\mathrm{Amdahl}} = 1/[(1-a) + a/s] - 1$, which asks how much faster a fixed body of work completes and is sharply concave in $a$. The two diverge fast: at $a = 0.5$ and $s = 3$, the task-output form gives $1.0$ and the serial-time form gives $0.5$---a factor of two, in the middle of the plausible range, purely from which question is being asked. This chapter uses the task-output form throughout, because research effort is reallocated rather than serialized: a group whose screening runs three times faster screens more, it does not go home early. Where the serial-time form is the right one---a single critical-path computation blocking a decision---Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-simple} overstates, and the funnel's conversion terms are doing none of that work.

The printed endpoint ranges then give $U_{\min}=0.15\times0.5\times0.20\times0.30=0.45\%$ and $U_{\max}=0.35\times4\times0.60\times0.70=58.8\%$; the gross converted output $U^{\mathrm{gross}}=asvd$ spans 1.35--73.5\%. The corner-to-corner interval is therefore roughly \emph{half a percent to sixty percent}, not 3--30\%, and the wider band is the honest one: it is what independence of the terms actually implies.

The corners, however, are not equally \emph{informative}, because the parameters are correlated in a specific, evidence-backed way: domains with high acceleration and high addressability (weather, structure prediction) also tend to have strong validation infrastructure and short deployment lags, while domains with weak validation (materials synthesis) also carry long deployment lags. It is worth being exact about what that correlation does, because the intuitive reading has the sign backwards. \emph{Positive} correlation among the conversion terms makes the joint corners \emph{more} likely, not less: if validation, deployment and lag move together, then the world in which all three are bad at once is a world the correlation structure actively favours. Correlation narrows the distribution's waist and fattens its tails; it does not make the tails unreachable. The corners are therefore retained as reachable scenario endpoints and are reported as such below. The right central formulation---and the one adopted here---is task-weighted:
\begin{equation}
U_{\mathrm{AI4S}} \;=\; \sum_j w_j\, a_j\,(s_j - 1)\, v_j\, d_j\, \ell_j,
\label{eq:funnel-weighted}
\end{equation}
where $j$ indexes task classes, $w_j$ is each class's share of global research effort, and $\ell_j$ is a lag-and-friction discount absorbing calendar delay, organizational adjustment, crowd-out, and regulatory latency---the term the single-pool version silently set to one. Table~\ref{tab:funnel-classes} states the class-level judgments [M] that the evidence of this chapter supports; this ``jagged scientific production function''---strong augmentation of search and prediction, weak automation of judgment---is the same structure the emerging economics literature on AI in science models formally~\cite{agrawal2026}.

A table of this kind fails silently if it omits the deployment column $d_j$---an omission that sets deployment conversion to one in the printed contributions without ever saying so---which is why the discipline here is that every published number be generated from published tuples. Table~\ref{tab:funnel-classes} complies: each $\Delta U_j$ is the exact product of its row, the central total is their exact sum (10.74\%), and the pessimistic/central/optimistic triple for every parameter lives in a machine-readable CSV shipped with the manuscript source, so any reader can recompute or re-weight the model in one spreadsheet. The simulation code and its seed ship with the source as well (\texttt{models/funnel-mc.py}, seed 20260923, 200{,}000 draws), so the percentiles below are not a claim about a computation the reader must take on trust; they are a computation the reader can re-run.

The Monte Carlo behind that table is run rather than promised, and it ships with the manuscript. Each parameter is sampled from a triangular distribution over its (pessimistic, central, optimistic) triple; the three conversion terms $(v_j, d_j, \ell_j)$ are coupled within and across classes through a single latent ``conversion-quality'' factor, implemented as a Gaussian copula with loading $\sqrt{0.5}$---the correlation structure the evidence supports, since domains with good validation also deploy faster---while $a_j$ and $s_j$ are drawn independently. Two hundred thousand draws at seed 20260923 give:

\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item central total from the printed tuples, \textbf{10.74\%};
  \item \textbf{median 11.61\%}; \textbf{P10--P90 6.73--18.98\%};
  \item deterministic corners: all-pessimistic \textbf{0.83\%}, all-optimistic \textbf{67.50\%}.
\end{itemize}

These percentiles are \emph{conditional on the assumed distributions}. They are not empirical confidence intervals and carry none of the coverage guarantees that phrase implies: they describe the spread of a model whose triangular marginals and correlation loading are author judgments [M], and a reader who rejects those judgments should re-run the file rather than discount the interval. The width of the band is a property of the assumptions, not evidence about the world.

One-at-a-time class sensitivity---each class swung across its own corner tuples with the others held central---reproduces exactly: prediction and surrogates 4.59--42.90\%, search and optimization 7.96--27.66\%, experimental planning 9.87--16.14\%, interpretation and causal 10.64--12.81\%, question selection and theory 10.74--10.96\%. The ordering is the quantitative form of this chapter's qualitative claim: the prize lives in prediction and search, and the two judgment-heavy classes cannot move the total no matter how generously they are parameterized. The headline is therefore restated once more, and more sharply than the endpoint arithmetic allowed: \emph{a task-weighted central estimate of $\approx$10.7\%, a model-conditional 10th-to-90th-percentile range of $\approx$6.7--19.0\%, and reachable all-pessimistic and all-optimistic scenario corners at 0.83\% and 67.5\%}. The commonly quoted ``3--30\%'' band survives only as a loose envelope: it contains the model-conditional range and is itself contained by the scenario corners. That is a weaker statement than the band is usually put to, and it is the one the arithmetic supports.

What may legitimately be concluded from the lower end, stated in the stock-and-flow discipline this chapter enforces (comparing a present value against an annual industry revenue mixes the two, and the temptation to do so is a standing one): the all-pessimistic corner of the task-weighted model is \textbf{0.83\%}---every class at its worst tuple simultaneously, which the positive correlation among the conversion terms makes a \emph{more} reachable world rather than a less reachable one---and $0.83\% \times \$3.1\mathrm{T} = \$25.7\mathrm{B}$ per year of incremental research-output flow. At the model-conditional 10th-percentile floor of 6.73\%, the same arithmetic gives \$209B per year. \emph{Even the lower end of the modeled uplift represents a socially consequential economic flow, and its present value---applying the Jones--Summers ratio to the increment---exceeds the cost of national AI4S infrastructure by a large multiple.} That is the quantitative form of this chapter's claim. What it does \emph{not} survive is the assumption that acceleration equals discovery, which is why the funnel is drawn.

\subsection{The measured analogue: what national-scale HPC actually returned}
\label{sec:hyperion}

The funnel's parameters are author judgments, and a fair reader will ask whether \emph{any} part of this value chain has ever been measured rather than modeled. One body of evidence exists, and it comes from the adjacent field with three decades of institutional practice: the ROI literature on national high-performance computing, which is the closest measured precedent for what AI4S economics will look like, because leadership HPC is the same asset class---publicly funded computational infrastructure applied to science---one technology generation earlier.

The most complete such study available to this author is Hyperion Research's multi-year assessment of RIKEN's national computing infrastructure and its planned successor~\cite{hyperion-roi}. Two disclosures belong directly beside that sentence: the study was \emph{commissioned by RIKEN}, and the author of this book directs the RIKEN center whose infrastructure it assesses. The reader should weigh everything below accordingly. Its grade is \textbf{[A/M]}, below a plain analyst estimate: what the study reports is not a direct causal measurement of the modeled AI4S quantity but \emph{a commissioned, survey-based, counterfactual economic-impact model informed partly by realized scientific projects}.

Its headline findings: Fugaku's COVID-19 response projects were estimated to have returned \$50--75B to the Japanese economy; 65.7\% of surveyed Fugaku researchers reported already-realized useful results on projects targeting major societal challenges; in an innovation-class mapping of more than 650 HPC projects worldwide, RIKEN's portfolio concentrated in the highest impact-and-importance classes; and the projected returns for the AI-capable successor machine---precisely because AI capability widens the addressable share $a_j$ and the acceleration $s_j$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-weighted}---were estimated at \$250B (pessimistic), \$400B+ (realistic), and \$600B+ (optimistic) over its life, two to four times Fugaku's, against an investment two orders of magnitude smaller. The same study put state-directed five-year AI commitments at \$100--200B for China against \$18--27B of US federal spending---the private-public asymmetry that Chapter~\ref{ch:trade} prices---and \$6--10B for Japan.

\begin{casestudy}{Methodology: what is observed, what is modeled, in an HPC ROI study}
Impact studies of this genre chain together components of very different evidentiary strength, and using one responsibly requires separating them. \emph{Observed}: that specific projects ran, produced outputs, and reached users---and beneficiary self-reports of usefulness (the 65.7\% figure is this class: real reports, subject to self-selection and social-desirability bias). \emph{Modeled}: the counterfactual (what would have happened without the machine); the attribution of outcomes to the computing infrastructure rather than to the researchers, data, and institutions around it; avoided-loss valuation (the COVID \$50--75B is this class---an estimate of economic collapse \emph{not} suffered, the least verifiable quantity in economics); spillover estimation; and all projected future benefits (the \$250--600B scenarios are projections, full stop). \emph{Unquantified risks}: double counting across projects, displacement (benefits that would have accrued elsewhere), deadweight (projects that needed no subsidy), and the absence of published confidence intervals. A commissioned study has, additionally, a selection gradient in what gets surveyed and how questions are framed. None of this makes the study worthless---it makes it [A/M], and it is why triangulation with independently governed evaluations matters: the UK's ARCHER2 evaluation, commissioned under UKRI's standard evaluation framework with explicit counterfactual scenarios and benefit-cost accounting against public investment, is the best current methodological benchmark for the genre~\cite{archer2-eval}, and a three-way Fugaku--ARCHER2--CERN-class comparison of evaluation methodologies is on this book's living-appendix work list. The claim this book actually rests on holds at that grade: national-scale computational infrastructure has repeatedly been \emph{evaluated}---by methods far from perfect but more disciplined than anything the enterprise-AI ROI literature offers---at returns that exceed costs by large multiples, concentrated in exactly the task classes where Table~\ref{tab:funnel-classes} puts the value.
\end{casestudy}

What the evidence is good for, at the grade it actually carries: it is \emph{partly ex post} (projects ran; users reported results), it is \emph{independent of this book's thesis} (the analysts were sizing a Japanese procurement, not arguing about China), and its \emph{ratio structure} matches the funnel---the reported value clusters in prediction, simulation, and search applied to societal-scale problems with operational deployment paths, not in the judgment-heavy classes where the funnel assigns near-zero. It is supporting evidence for the funnel's shape and order of magnitude, not a measurement of its value.

\begin{casestudy}{Weather forecasting, end to end through the funnel}
Weather is the one domain where every term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-weighted} can be observed, which is why it recurs throughout this book and deserves assembly in one place. \emph{Addressability}: the forward model is the dominant computational cost of forecasting, and it is exactly what AI replaced---AIFS operational at ECMWF from February 2025, NOAA's AI system from December 2025 at 0.3\% of the compute of its conventional suite~\cite{ecmwf-aifs,noaa-ai}. \emph{Acceleration}: $10^3$--$10^5\times$ per forecast, measured, not asserted~\cite{aurora,neuralgcm}. \emph{Validation}: forecasting possesses the strongest validation infrastructure in all of science---daily scoring against observation, decades of baseline skill curves---so survival is measured within months. \emph{Deployment}: operational integration inside a year, because national agencies already own the delivery channel. And then, in May 2026, the term everyone forgets: ECMWF discontinued real-time running of all four \emph{external} AI models after an upgrade to its analysis system degraded several of them, while its own AIFS---coupled to its own data assimilation---remained operational~\cite{ecmwf-farewell}. The episode demonstrates five things at once: an AI forecast model is a \emph{component of a production system}, not a free-standing oracle; its performance is contingent on upstream data-assimilation infrastructure it does not control; retraining against infrastructure change is a recurring operational cost, not a one-time expense; deployment requires institutional continuity of exactly the kind Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} argues must be built rather than rented; and openness was what made the failure \emph{visible}---the retired models could be evaluated, compared, and learned from precisely because they were published. Weather is thus not merely the best AI4S success story; it is the complete systems lesson---architecture, operations, lifecycle cost, trust, and value conversion in one worked example---and the standard against which every other domain's claims in this chapter should be read.
\end{casestudy}

\subsection{The funnel at volume: mathematics in October 2026}
\label{sec:sci-october-release}

Weather shows every term of the funnel converting. Mathematics in October 2026 shows what happens when the first term is run at a volume the later terms were never sized for. It is the largest natural experiment on Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-simple} yet conducted, and on one point its result is unambiguous: \emph{output is not discovery, and verification, not generation, is the binding stage}.

\emph{The release.} On 6 October OpenAI published 722 manuscripts, grouped into 372 ``result families,'' in a public repository, produced by a system it describes only as an ``unreleased internal model''~\cite{sci-rw-openai} [P]. The count itself is unstable---one newspaper tally gives 377 findings, and 719 manuscripts remained after withdrawals [A]---which is the first sign that the unit of output is not the unit of discovery. The release followed a recommendation from the Advisory Group on Mathematics and AI, hosted at the Institute for Advanced Study, to publish without waiting for full formalization [A]. The validation term began to bite within a day. On 7 October OpenAI withdrew three manuscripts after a sign error broke an argument about Weil classes and took two dependent K3-surface papers down with it, and revised fourteen more; the company told Retraction Watch that about half the results had been released unconfirmed, and about 42\% had been formalized in Lean~\cite{sci-rw-openai,sci-sciam-openai} [A]. Seventeen of 722 manuscripts, 2.4\%, were withdrawn or revised in the first twenty-four hours [M, arithmetic on the reported counts]. That is not a survival rate---none has been measured---but it is a floor on the attrition the funnel predicts, observed before any external referee had finished reading.

\emph{A Lean check is not the stated theorem.} The sharpest finding of the month concerns the formal layer itself. OpenAI's Navier--Stokes blow-up claim of 8 September came with a Lean formalization; on 9 October a team led by Anders Hansen showed that the Lean code proves weaker statements than the written proof in places---in Lemma~8.6 the paper's bound ``$<m+4$'' becomes ``$<m+5$'' in Lean---and proposed that auto-formalization drifts toward code that compiles~\cite{sci-navier-lean} [A]. OpenAI acknowledged the mismatch. Kevin Buzzard said he was confident the problem had been solved and ``much less sure the text proof is correct''; the Clay Institute still lists the problem as open [A]. The mechanism matters more than the instance. Formal verification certifies that a formal statement follows from formal axioms; it does not certify that the formal statement is the theorem the paper claims, and that second step is a human judgement of fidelity that no proof assistant performs. In mathematics the validation term $v_j$ is therefore a product of two factors, and the cheaper of the two is the one that has been automated. A generator rewarded for passing the first will, on the Hansen hypothesis, erode the second.

\emph{The most-praised result is the least autonomous.} The release's strongest result is a quasi-Riemann theorem: every nontrivial zero of the zeta function lies in the strip $1/12 \le \mathrm{Re}(s) \le 11/12$. An earlier $1/8$--$7/8$ version, dated 30 September, is Lean-verified; the $11/12$ version, dated 5 October, is not, and it is the only paper in the release that a human---unnamed---edited~\cite{sci-quanta-qrh} [P/A]. \emph{Quanta}'s assessment of 19 October carries the field's reaction across its full range: Lichtman called it ``the most significant AI-for-math achievement so far, by quite a margin''; Radziwi\l\l{} found the implications ``staggering'' and the papers poorly written; Sarnak declined to call it a theorem until humans have checked the details; and it is not peer-reviewed~\cite{sci-quanta-qrh} [A]. The strongest version of the most-praised result is the one that is unformalized and human-edited. On the October record, the result the field values most is the one with a human in its chain---which inverts the autonomy narrative that accompanied the release.

\emph{Claims still at the first stage.} On 26 October OpenAI announced three further results~\cite{sci-openai-oct26} [P]: a proof of the local smoothing conjecture in all dimensions, generated on 18 October and formalized in Lean the next day, which the company says implies the Kakeya and restriction conjectures; a disproof of the Exponential Time Hypothesis, generated on 16 October and formalized on 17 October, which would not by itself yield faster algorithms; and existence proofs for interacting four-dimensional gauge theories including pure Yang--Mills and QCD, a question related to a Clay problem. None is independently verified as of \datafreeze. If one survives, it is the largest AI-for-science claim yet made; a formalization completed within a day of generation is, after Navier--Stokes, a statement about formal code and not yet about the theorems. The durable novelty is institutional. OpenAI named external review panels---Impagliazzo, Paturi and Ryan Williams for the complexity result; Gross, Douglas, Jaffe, Klebanov and Nekrasov, who will write their own account before the materials are released, for the gauge theories---and will fund a conference, on top of an earlier \$5M pledge to the Simons Foundation for workshops on this work [P]. \emph{The producer is now paying for the validation stage.} On the same day Springer Nature and Google DeepMind announced a partnership to co-design a Gemini-based AI peer-review assistant~\cite{sci-springer-gdm} [P]. Within one month two of the leading Western laboratories moved to supply the stage of the funnel that binds, which is the certification argument of Chapter~\ref{ch:trust} arriving in the scientific literature: whoever operates verification will set the terms on which machine output becomes knowledge.

\emph{The peer-reviewed baseline.} The month's only result to pass formal peer review is Google DeepMind's AlphaProof Nexus, published in \emph{Science} on 8 October~\cite{sci-alphaproof-nexus} [V]: 9 of 353 Lean-formalized Erd\H{o}s problems solved, a 2.5\% hit rate, including Erd\H{o}s--S\'ark\"ozy problem~12, open since 1970; 44 of 492 OEIS conjectures; a fifteen-year-old Hilbert-function conjecture; and a problem from a list of Ben Green's. The method couples Gemini~3.1 Pro to a Lean feedback loop, with AlphaProof reserved for hard subproblems, at up to 3,000 rounds and several hundred dollars of inference per problem. Its own ablation is the most informative line in the paper: \emph{a plain language model with Lean feedback also solved all nine}. An independent audit trims the result further: problem \#846 already follows from prior work, so a strict count is eight; \#26 is a variant Erd\H{o}s did not pose; and the statements of \#125 and \#741(i) were amended~\cite{sci-papex-erdos} [A]. Thomas Bloom judged the results ``not really at the top level of importance'' [A]. Three readings follow. The hit rate sits where this chapter's ledger already placed the tractable fraction---Tao's 1--2\% and the 6.5\% meaningfully correct share of one adjudicated corpus~\cite{tao,aletheia}---so peer review moves that fraction from estimate to measurement without moving it much. The ablation says the specialized prover was unnecessary for these nine: the verification loop, not the generator's architecture, carried the result. And no authoritative tally of AI-solved Erd\H{o}s problems exists---one community wiki, frozen on 30 June, counted 69 with an AI-led full solution, 35 of them with significant human collaboration, while OpenAI's release covers 25, 21 of them formalized~\cite{sci-papex-erdos} [A]---so the field cannot yet state its own output in a common unit, let alone its return.

\emph{October, read through the funnel.} Mapped onto Eq.~\eqref{eq:funnel-weighted}, the month is a demonstration of this section's second and third disciplines on a single task class. The acceleration term $s_j$ for search-class mathematics was very large---hundreds of manuscripts in weeks---and it converted into withdrawal, revision, formal--informal divergence and a review backlog, not into an equal volume of discovery. The binding stage is $v_j$, and its two factors scale differently: formal checking scales with compute, fidelity checking scales with expert-hours, and the release multiplied the demand on the second without adding to its supply. In the production metric of Section~\ref{sec:sci-metric}, the release scores high on the volume component of $\mathbf{B}^{\uparrow}$ and transfers $C_{\mathrm{assurance}}$ in $\mathbf{C}^{\downarrow}$ and the false-discovery burden in $\mathbf{R}^{\downarrow}$ to a community that did not produce it---incomparable on the Pareto rule, and expensive at any published trade-off weight. The October record does not measure $v_j$ and moves no parameter of Table~\ref{tab:funnel-classes} [M]; it confirms the table's structure, in which the search class's contribution is bounded by validation and deployment rather than by speed. The competitive reading carries equal weight: the October output, whatever its survival rate, was produced on closed American compute by a model nobody outside its laboratory can run, and it bears on the substitutability condition for P3 at the frontier tier of scientific work, not on P1.

\emph{The split among mathematicians.} The reception divided along a line this book's thesis cares about. In the West the profession mobilized against the laboratories: the Leiden Declaration of June gathered more than 2,600 signatories and the endorsement of the International Mathematical Union; after the Navier--Stokes announcement more than 8,000 researchers endorsed concerns that it was rushed, and on 11 September Tao and 24 other Fields Medallists issued the declaration ``A Severe Misalignment of AI in Mathematics''; on 7 October the Association for Human Mathematics called the mass release ``a demonstration of power'' and urged mathematicians to stop working with OpenAI~\cite{sci-sciam-openai,sci-rw-openai} [A]. In Beijing on 11 October Shing-Tung Yau launched the Yau Laboratory Alliance for Mathematics and AI---eight institutions and a planned network of more than 1,000 scientists, aimed at building specialized mathematical models---saying ``A year ago, I did not think AI was this powerful,'' calling it ``like a tsunami,'' and adding that ``Tao \ldots\ does not represent the mathematical community''~\cite{sci-yau-alliance,sci-officechai} [A]. Western mathematicians are organizing against the producers; Chinese mathematicians are organizing to integrate the method. The reading for the thesis is that the adoption asymmetry Part~II documents in software has a counterpart in scientific practice. The reading against it is in Yau's own words: if Chinese models were asked to do what OpenAI's had done in the preceding month, ``the gap is very large''~\cite{sci-yau-alliance} [A]. The Western objections, read closely, concern conduct and sequencing rather than the method---Andrew Sutherland of MIT called the withdrawal responsible while reporting that ``many mathematicians [are] unhappy with OpenAI's conduct,'' and Alex Townsend of Cornell argued that the Lean-verified results should have been released first~\cite{sci-rw-openai} [A]---and a boycott call is not a measured withdrawal. The alliance is an announcement of mandate, not a shipped artifact, and Chapter~\ref{ch:china-ai4s} scores it as such.

\emph{What the 2026 Nobel prizes do and do not say.} The Physiology or Medicine prize of 5 October went to Deisseroth, Hegemann and Nagel for light-gated ion channels and optogenetics; the Physics prize of 6 October to Halzen for the IceCube Neutrino Observatory and the discovery of high-energy astrophysical neutrinos; the Chemistry prize of 7 October to Kagan and Soai for non-linear effects and autocatalysis in asymmetric organic synthesis. None has an AI component, and press coverage of all three carried no AI mention~\cite{sci-nobel-2026} [V]. What that means: any claim of a continuing AI Nobel streak after 2024 is false, and the 2024 prizes remain the only instances of the highest scientific recognition attaching to AI work~\cite{nobel-2024}. What it does not mean: a prize committee decorates results whose consequences it regards as settled, so the absence of AI from the 2026 list is not evidence against the results this chapter credits---any more than the 2024 chemistry prize was evidence for the October output. The useful contrast is with Chapter~\ref{ch:china-ai4s}: China's top science award went to a computing machine; the Nobel committees, in the month of the largest AI mathematics release on record, went to experiments. Neither is a verdict on AI for science. Both are statements about what each institution considers settled enough to honour.

\subsection{Pricing the funnel before it converts: drugs and data}
\label{sec:sci-pricing-funnel}

Capital markets and data institutions run the same funnel and discount it differently, and October supplied one clean instance of each.

\emph{Drugs.} On 8 October Bloomberg reported that Alphabet's Isomorphic Labs was in early talks to raise at a valuation of at least \$40B, possibly \$50B, after a \$2.1B Series~B closed in May; the company ``hasn't dosed a single patient,'' and its first trials, once targeted for the end of 2025, are targeted for the end of 2026~\cite{sci-isomorphic} [A]. The most clinically advanced AI-discovered drug, by contrast, is rentosertib, from a company with Chinese and US operations, whose Phase~III dosed its first patient on 10 September at Chinese centres~\cite{sci-insilico-dosing} [P]. In the funnel's terms a valuation is the market's estimate of $a\,(s-1)\,v\,d\,\ell$ for one firm's pipeline, and at zero patients dosed the market is pricing $v$ and $d$ entirely on expectation. The juxtaposition contradicts any premise that AlphaFold-lineage drug design is already in the clinic. The case for the valuation is also real: it may price a platform, its partnerships and an option on many programmes rather than any single molecule, and rentosertib remains one drug, one surrogate endpoint and a Phase~III that has not read out.

\emph{Data.} On 7 October the Biohub ``virtual biology'' initiative reached \$1.8B with DOE, NIH, Google DeepMind, Isomorphic and Meta: DOE more than \$500M over five years for an ``AI-ready open data resource''; NIH a matching contribution in kind, in datasets and repositories built on more than \$500M of prior federal spending; and \$300M combined from the three companies, with participants including the Broad, Sanger, the Human Cell Atlas and the Human Protein Atlas~\cite{sci-biohub} [P/A]. The named components sum to \$1.3B; the remaining \$0.5B is not itemized. Biohub's head of science, Alex Rives, confirmed ``a one-year embargo'': commercial funders see the industry-funded data a year before the public, on licence terms not disclosed~\cite{sci-biohub-embargo} [A]. Three observations follow. A data initiative, not a model, is among the largest scientific items of the 8 October summit package, which is where Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium}'s data-stewardship pillar says the money should go. The embargo is the layered data policy of that chapter implemented with time as the price: industry buys a year of priority over a resource that public money co-funds, and whether the price is fair cannot be judged while the licence is undisclosed. And it marks the two commons now under construction as different in kind: the Chinese commons of Chapter~\ref{ch:china-ai4s} is built from weights released to everyone at once; the American one is being built from data, with priority sold to its funders. The first is a means of production released without conditions; the second is the input to $v_j$ for every prediction-class model in biology, released on a schedule.

\section{Measuring the scientific frontier: a production metric, not another benchmark}
\label{sec:sci-metric}

Chapter~\ref{ch:model} left the scientific frontier as the one frontier without an accepted metric, and noting a vacancy is worth less than proposing something to fill it. Benchmarks will not serve: a score on a question-answering set measures a component, and the failure ledger above is a catalogue of components that scored well while the system failed. What a ministry actually needs is a \emph{production} objective---what came out, against what went in, how long it took, and what risk it carried.

The obvious way to write that objective is the wrong way, and the error is worth naming because it is the one every attempt at this metric makes. Putting all the outcome measures into a single numerator over a cost denominator mixes quantities whose good direction is opposite: replication survival, novelty and downstream adoption are higher-is-better, while time to result, false-discovery burden and dual-use externality are lower-is-better. A ratio built that way rewards a programme for taking longer and for generating more false positives, and no amount of reporting the numerator as a vector repairs it, because a reader comparing two vectors still has to know which way each component points. Worse, expert-hours consumed appears in both places at once---as an outcome measure and as $C_{\mathrm{human}}$---so a programme that substitutes compute for expert labour is charged for the saving and credited for it in the same expression. The result is a metric on which a better outcome can score worse by construction, which is the one property a production objective may not have.

The corrected form separates the four blocks and declares each one's orientation:
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}} \;=\; \bigl(\; \mathbf{B}^{\uparrow};\;\; \mathbf{C}^{\downarrow};\;\; \mathbf{T}^{\downarrow};\;\; \mathbf{R}^{\downarrow} \;\bigr),
\label{eq:sci-metric}
\end{equation}
evaluated as an expectation over the portfolio of projects undertaken, and reported as four blocks rather than as one number.

\emph{Benefits} $\mathbf{B}^{\uparrow}$, higher is better: replication survival rate; novelty and importance (no single automated novelty metric survives axiomatic scrutiny, and combined metrics do better~\cite{liu2026novelty}); downstream adoption; breadth across domains.

\emph{Costs} $\mathbf{C}^{\downarrow}$, lower is better and in currency: $C_{\mathrm{compute}}$ the machine time, $C_{\mathrm{human}}$ the expert labour---which is where expert-hours live, and \emph{only} where they live---$C_{\mathrm{experimental}}$ the wet-lab and instrument cost, $C_{\mathrm{data}}$ acquisition and curation, and $C_{\mathrm{assurance}}$ the verification and certification overhead of Chapter~\ref{ch:trust}. These five are the cost stack of Chapter~\ref{ch:theory} specialized to science, and the $C_{\mathrm{assurance}}$ term is where the trust stack enters the economics of discovery directly.

\emph{Times} $\mathbf{T}^{\downarrow}$, lower is better: time to independently validated result; time to operational or regulatory deployment. Kept out of the cost block because calendar and money are not interchangeable at any fixed rate, and out of the benefit block because shortening them is an improvement.

\emph{Risks} $\mathbf{R}^{\downarrow}$, lower is better: false-discovery burden; safety and dual-use externality.

Two comparison rules follow, and a programme must state which it is using. \emph{Pareto comparison} is the default and needs no weights: system $A$ beats system $B$ only if it is at least as good on every component of all four blocks and strictly better on one. Most real comparisons will be incomparable under this rule, and saying so is informative---it is the honest report that a faster result was bought with more false positives, which the single-ratio form would have hidden inside a quotient. \emph{Declared trade-off} is the fallback where a decision must be made anyway, and it prices the trade explicitly:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{S}_{\pi} \;=\; \frac{\sum_b \pi_b B_b}{\sum_c C_c \;+\; \sum_t \tau_t T_t \;+\; \sum_r \kappa_r R_r},
\label{eq:sci-metric-scalar}
\end{equation}
where $\pi$, $\tau$ and $\kappa$ are published exchange rates---currency per unit of benefit, per month of delay, per unit of false-discovery burden. Every term of Eq.~\eqref{eq:sci-metric-scalar} points the same way: a better result cannot lower the score. The weights are contestable, which is the point of publishing them; the single-ratio form's weights are unwritten and equal to one.

The recent measurement literature supports both the vector form and the humility. The AlphaFold evidence shows \emph{complementarity}---AI-using researchers produced 40\% more novel experimental structures, not fewer---so a metric that scored substitution of human effort would have missed the largest effect~\cite{hill2026,alphafold-impact}. Frontier agents remain below 10\% on demanding scientific literature-discovery tasks, so any metric weighted toward autonomous end-to-end discovery would currently measure noise~\cite{xiong2026}. Scientific value arises from a coupled human--model--simulation--experiment system, and Eq.~\eqref{eq:sci-metric} is written over that system, not over the model. The strategic consequence: whoever operationalizes $\boldsymbol{\mathcal{S}}$ first---runs it across a national research portfolio, publishes the vector, iterates procurement against it---owns the scientific-frontier scoreboard the way MLPerf owns the training scoreboard, and Appendix~\ref{app:dashboard} adds its adoption to the forecast register.

\section{The architecture of the prize}
\label{sec:prize-arch}

The failure ledger and the architecture argument of Part~II point at the same conclusion. AI4S succeeds where a capable model is coupled to simulation, instruments, and data at scale---weather models coupled to assimilation systems, structure prediction coupled to experimental validation, design models coupled to autonomous laboratories. It fails where a model is asked to supply judgement alone. The workload that follows is therefore not chat: it is \emph{long-context agentic orchestration over large batch scientific execution}, which is precisely the two-layer demand structure that Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing} measures.

That structure is what makes scientific AI a different procurement from consumer or enterprise AI, and the distinction is worth carrying explicitly, because a claim about one is not evidence about the other. Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing} labels three demand regimes (Section~\ref{sec:regimes}): Regime~G, general and consumer AI through phones and applications, and Regime~E, enterprise streamlining of accounting, personnel and approvals, are both essentially all LLM token generation---agents in them call public shared services such as web search, not privately provisioned high-end compute---and they are what the United States and China contest head to head. Regime~S, the subject of this chapter, is dominated by non-LLM work: simulation, training of models that are not language models, data processing at instrument rate, and integration with physical apparatus, with a token layer for orchestration that is a minority of the requirement and, on the serving-generation scenario of Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing}, shrinking from about a seventh toward a twentieth. Within that minority token layer the cost is dominated by million-token-context master orchestration rather than by raw dense FLOPs, which is why the architecture conclusion below follows even though the layer is small.

Three consequences follow, each connecting to an earlier chapter. \emph{First}, the hardware that serves this best is capacity-rich and bandwidth-balanced rather than FLOP-maximal---the design point of Section~\ref{sec:lpddr6}, and the reason the memory arbitrage is a scientific-infrastructure argument and not only an economic one. \emph{Second}, the models that serve it best are open, because scientific work requires reproducibility, provenance, auditability, and the freedom to fine-tune on data that cannot leave the institution---and because, as Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} showed, buying the orchestration layer entirely at a 20--60$\times$ premium turns a token-service layer of 14.6\% of Regime~S physical workload into 77.4--91.1\% of the budget, with selective routing of only a tenth of that layer bringing the same figure down to 33.1--54.1\%---and, on the serving-generation scenario of Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing}, to 48.5--73.8\% and 12.0--24.5\% respectively, which lowers every figure without changing which way the argument runs. \emph{Third}, the failure modes are exactly the ones Chapters~\ref{ch:trust} and~\ref{ch:order66} catalogue: a scientific result produced by an unverifiable artifact through an unauditable chain is not a scientific result, which makes the certification layer a precondition of the prize rather than an accessory to it.

\section{Who is positioned}

The uncomfortable observation is developed in full in Chapter~\ref{ch:china-ai4s}, which reads the Chinese policy record precisely, measures the output through a European Commission bibliometric study rather than a Chinese source, and finds a programme organized around infrastructure and industrial delivery rather than around discovery. The short form: the most industrially organized AI-for-science programme in the world is Chinese, a substantial part of its output is given away under permissive licences, and the state's top science prize in the most recent cycle went to a domestic computing machine rather than to a discovery made on one~\cite{ec-ai4s-biblio,pengcheng-cloudbrain}.

Set against this, the Western response is real and large: a US national mission with an executive-order mandate to double the productivity of American science within a decade, over \$5 billion of federal commitment across fifteen-plus agencies, extended at the White House summit of 8 October into a package of more than \$6 billion that includes \$2.4 billion of mostly in-kind industry pledges (Section~\ref{sec:sci-gap-test}), a cohort counted at 278 projects in July and at 297 teams in October~\cite{sci-wh-summit,sci-doe-phase2} [V], and the two largest scientific AI systems ever procured~\cite{genesis,genesis-scale,genesis-projects}; nineteen European AI factories with a €10 billion envelope and a €20 billion gigafactory programme behind it~\cite{eu-factories,eu-gigafactories}; national systems in the UK and Japan explicitly mandated for AI-driven science~\cite{isambard,fugakunext}. The contest is joined. But note which side of it is being fought with open weights and cheap tokens, and recall from Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing} that the announced infrastructures on \emph{both} sides underprovision their own research populations by roughly an order of magnitude.

\section{The strategic restatement}

If AI4S is even a major pathway with the spillovers Section~\ref{sec:denominator} allows---and the ranking in this chapter's title is the part that is \emph{not} established---then three of this book's arguments change register.

The token-price argument becomes a \emph{compounding-rate} argument. Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium} showed that a token-service layer bought entirely at a 20--60$\times$ premium consumes 77.4--91.1\% of an AI-for-science budget, and that a competent institution routing selectively pays 33.1--54.1\% for the same science. Read against a 5\%-per-year decline in research productivity and a social return above \$4 per dollar, the difference between those two is not a procurement inefficiency---it is a tax on an activity whose returns compound into the means of making everything else, including the next generation of AI. A nation that pays the full premium runs its science at a fraction of the rate the same money would buy.

The commoditization thesis acquires a beneficiary. Everything Part~II describes---open weights, cheap inference, capacity-first hardware, sovereign-scale clusters---is, from the perspective of this chapter, \emph{good}. Abundant cheap capable AI applied to research is the best available answer to the ideas-getting-harder problem, whoever supplies it. The book's warning is not that commoditization is bad; it is that the country organizing the commons acquires the standards, the adoption, the certification authority, and the scientific compounding that come with it.

And the strategy for everyone else sharpens to a single sentence. If the largest value in AI is scientific, and scientific AI requires abundant compute, open models, verifiable artifacts, and institutions that can carry multi-year continuity, then the correct national investment is not a sovereign chatbot. It is measured capacity (Chapter~\ref{ch:sizing}), pooled internationally (Chapter~\ref{ch:consortium}), running audited open models (Chapter~\ref{ch:trust}) on architectures chosen for long-context agentic science (Chapter~\ref{ch:compute}). That is the whole recommendation of this book, and this chapter is the reason it is worth the money.
